# Critical payment alert with a healthy payment path: a telemetry-visibility failure

## What we were handed

The page named paymentservice, severity critical, with a stated blast radius of three services and two edges that nobody had measured. Onset was recorded at T+0 (the alert timestamp). That framing is worth remembering, because almost everything that followed pointed away from payments actually failing, and the record never closed the gap between the alert and the evidence.

The first instinct in the chair was the obvious one: a critical alert on payments means charges are failing. That instinct was wrong, and it cost time.

## First look: logs on paymentservice

Around T+3m we pulled the payment-service log stream for the half hour leading into onset. It was uniformly boring. Every returned line was info-level and consisted of the normal charge-request / transaction-complete pair. There were no error lines, no warnings, no exceptions, no stack traces — not just near onset, but anywhere in the returned portion.

Charges completed on both sides of the alert: a successful transaction roughly three seconds before onset, another about seven seconds after, and then a steady cadence continuing for the next two minutes. So payments were working while a critical payment alert was open.

Two details from this pass are worth carrying forward. First, there is a roughly nine-second interval around onset with no log lines at all — a quiet gap, not an error burst. Second, the hostname and pid on the oldest returned lines differ from the newest, which is what a container replacement looks like. The middle of the window was truncated, so we could not date the replacement, and we never went back to pin it down. If you are re-treading this, that is a loose thread: it may well be the mechanism by which a new environment value took effect.

## Dead end: looking for the error signal in metrics

With logs clean, the next move was the error-ratio series for paymentservice, derived from span-based call counters. This is where the investigation went sideways in a useful way.

The query came back empty. Not zero — empty. No samples in the incident window at all. So we widened to a roughly three-hour baseline before onset, expecting to see a healthy series that stopped at onset. The baseline was equally empty.

That killed two hypotheses at once. It killed the idea that a collection outage began at onset, because there was nothing to lose. And it killed any temptation to read the empty result as "paymentservice was clean" — an absent series is not a nominal reading, and paymentservice cannot be exonerated on error volume from a measurement that never happened.

It also raised the question the record never answered: if that series has no samples in either window, it cannot have fired the alert. Whatever signal tripped at onset came from somewhere we did not find.

Worth stating plainly for the next responder: this pass also returned nothing for request rate, latency percentiles, CPU, memory, or restart counts on paymentservice. Those dimensions are unmeasured, not excluded. Resource pressure on the service was never actually ruled out.

## The caller's view

If payments were failing in a way paymentservice was not reporting, the caller should have felt it. Around T+12m we checked checkoutservice's error ratio over the incident window against the preceding baseline.

Flat zero, both windows, with a full complement of samples in each and no variance. The denominator carried samples throughout, which also tells us checkoutservice was receiving real traffic and had an intact telemetry pipeline — a useful contrast with paymentservice.

This ruled out checkoutservice as the origin, and ruled out any downstream payment failure propagating back to it as error-status spans. The caveat: a failure swallowed without error status would look identical. But combined with the payment logs showing completed transactions, the simpler reading held.

## Traces: partly useful, partly a trap

The trace backend did return paymentservice spans across the queried window. In each of five distinct traces, a paymentservice server span for the Charge RPC sat correctly under the checkoutservice Charge call, with its own child charge span beneath it. Propagation was intact; nothing orphaned or truncated.

The spans were also fast — Charge around 0.2ms against an enclosing order-placement span of 22–26ms, with the bulk of that time in the shipping-quote path. So payments were not the latency source for checkout either.

The trap: we wanted to use this to answer "do paymentservice spans stop arriving after the change?" We could not. The result was capped at 200 spans across only five trace ids, with no per-span timestamps returned. It cannot place any span before or after the change. That question remains open, and anyone re-running it should query with explicit time bounds and a much wider trace-id spread.

## The change

The change log for paymentservice in the causal window contained exactly one entry: an environment variable update applied by platform automation roughly five minutes before onset, setting the OTLP traces exporter endpoint from unset to a loopback address (127.0.0.1:4317).

No collector listens on loopback from inside the payment container. Spans emitted by paymentservice are therefore shipped to a dead local endpoint.

Because there was only one entry, several of the usual candidates fell away without needing separate work: no code deploy or image rollout, no feature-flag flip, no credential or secret rotation, no dependency version bump. There was also no attribution ambiguity requiring bisection. And the timing was tight enough that it could not be dismissed on chronology — five minutes ahead of onset sits squarely in the window.

Note what the change touches: telemetry export configuration only. Not payment logic, not routing, not data paths. That is consistent with everything else we saw — the request path kept working while the observability signal did not.

## Where we landed

The failing mechanism is a telemetry-visibility failure on paymentservice, caused by an exporter endpoint pointed at a dead loopback address. The service's own charge path kept working throughout: charges completed either side of onset with no error output, and the captured Charge spans were fast and correctly parented. The caller saw nothing. The critical alert reflects the disappearance of paymentservice's observability signal rather than customer-visible payment errors.

Fix class is a config revert of the exporter endpoint.

Confidence is medium, deliberately. The argument is coherent but it does not close.

## What is still open

Read this section before you trust the conclusion.

What actually fired the alert is unknown. The paymentservice error-ratio series it would most obviously derive from has no samples in either window, so the alerting signal came from a source we never identified. Find the alert definition first if this recurs.

The metric gap is not onset-correlated. The three-hour baseline is equally empty, which predates the environment change by hours. Either paymentservice never had a call-counter series under that name, or the exporter was already misdirected earlier. The loopback value alone does not explain the baseline emptiness — this is the weakest joint in the story.

Whether paymentservice spans stop arriving in the trace backend after the change is unresolved, for the capping and timestamp reasons above.

The container replacement implied by the hostname/pid shift has no established time or cause, and we never confirmed whether it was how the new environment value took effect.

No saturation, latency, restart-count or request-rate data was collected for paymentservice at all.

Finally: triage recorded two unmeasured edges and a third affected service that no dispatch ever examined. Whatever symptom made this critical across three services is unaccounted for. If you are here again, start there rather than re-walking the payment logs.
