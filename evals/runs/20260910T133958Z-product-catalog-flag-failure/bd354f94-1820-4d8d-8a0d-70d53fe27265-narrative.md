# Product catalog error bursts with an unresolved mechanism

## What we saw first

Paging came from three places at once — frontend, loadgenerator and productcatalogservice — with a blast radius nominally covering seven services and a critical severity. The instinct from that page order was to treat frontend as the origin, since it alerted first and is the customer-facing surface. That instinct was wrong and cost the first stretch of the investigation.

Take T+0 as the declared onset (13:43:30 in wall clock). Everything below is offset from there.

## Dead end: frontend as origin

The frontend error-ratio comparison came back inverted from expectation. Over the incident window the mean sat around 1% with a peak near 8.8%, while the immediately preceding baseline window had a mean near 5.2% and a peak above 32%. Frontend was erroring roughly five times *less* during the incident than in its own quiet period, and no sustained departure from baseline was flagged. Whatever elevated frontend errors existed happened before the window opened, not near T+0.

A second limitation surfaced here and is worth recording for whoever picks this up: the frontend metric is aggregated only by service name. There is no route, span-name or downstream-peer dimension, so you cannot rank dependencies by failure contribution from this metric at all. Any per-peer attribution has to come from trace or span data. Frontend was a detector, not a cause.

## The one genuinely new signal

productcatalogservice is the only service in scope whose behaviour is demonstrably new. Against a three-hour baseline the error ratio was exactly zero — 728 samples, minimum, maximum, mean and standard deviation all zero. During the incident it became non-zero, with a peak near 8.9%. A single change point was detected, at T-2m, where the ratio crossed the 5% threshold.

The shape matters. Incident-window minimum is still zero, and the standard deviation (~0.026) exceeds the mean (~0.010), so these are short bursts rather than a steady failure rate. The service was serving successfully for the large majority of samples. That rules out a hard-down state, a crash-loop-with-total-outage picture, and any "chronic problem we only just noticed" reading — the zero-variance baseline forecloses all three.

One honest tension in the record: read against the wider three-hour window, the change point falls in the final minutes, which reads like a late-stage symptom rather than a trigger. Read against the tighter window, it is clearly a fresh onset two minutes ahead of the declared start. Both readings were derived from the same query. The reconciliation is unfinished, and a responder should not treat productcatalogservice's primacy as settled ordering.

## Dead end: the automation cycles on cartservice and adservice

Two neighbouring services carried change activity that looked promising and turned out to explain nothing.

cartservice had twenty entries in the twenty-four-hour change log, all attributed to platform-automation, all forming matched apply/revert triples: an image reference set then reverted, a REDIS_ADDR override pointing at redis-cart on port 6380 instead of the default, and a traffic-shaping sidecar attached to the network namespace imposing a fixed 300ms egress delay with zero jitter. Every element of that triple was already undone before onset — the sidecar removed about 1.2h prior, the image reverted about 1.6h prior, the REDIS_ADDR variable returned to unset about 57 minutes prior. The same triple recurs four times across the window on a roughly six-to-seven-hour cadence, including cycles hours earlier that produced no incident at all. That recurrence is what killed the correlation.

adservice had twelve entries, again all platform-automation, again all one change type: a memory limit lowered to 256m and then restored, six matched pairs across the preceding ~23h. The pair nearest onset began about 1.9h before and was reverted about 1.8h before, so no reduced limit was in force at T+0. No deployments, no config edits, no flag flips appear for adservice at all.

Both were checked precisely because they were the most mechanically plausible stories available. Both were fully reverted well before onset and both recur harmlessly on a schedule. Record them as excluded, not unexamined.

## Dead end: no local change on the suspect service

The change-log query for productcatalogservice over the full twenty-four hours came back empty. No deployments, no configuration edits, no feature-flag transitions are registered against the service for the period ending at onset. There is no release boundary to correlate with anything.

The useful inference is the negative one: with no local change activity, the trigger is more likely external to productcatalogservice — an upstream or downstream change, a datastore, a load shift or a data shift — so the search should have widened from here rather than stopped.

## Dead end: the log query that proved nothing

One Loki query was run against productcatalogservice over the ~32-minute window covering onset. It returned zero lines. The selector used a hyphenated service label value that does not match the unhyphenated service name under investigation, which is the likely reason for the empty result rather than a genuine absence of logs. The result was explicitly empty, not truncated, so result-set clipping is not the explanation either.

Treat this as no information at all. It does not tell you whether the service panicked, restarted, or logged dependency errors around onset. It does tell you that this selector is not a working handle on the stream and should not be reused. Re-query with the correct label first thing.

## Where it was left, and what to do next

Conclusion as recorded: productcatalogservice is the only service carrying a genuinely new failure signal, and it is named as the locus. The mechanism is unresolved and confidence is low. No fix class was identified.

Three gaps, in the order I would attack them:

First, no latency, CPU, memory or container-restart series was ever returned for productcatalogservice. Both metric queries returned only the error-ratio template. Memory-pressure kills and crash-looping remain completely untested — not ruled out, untested.

Second, no logs were actually read. Re-run the log query with the correct unhyphenated label and look for panics and downstream errors.

Third, the unmeasured edge on the graph was never crossed. productcatalogservice's own datastore and peers have no evidence against them, and four of the seven services in the blast radius received no dispatch whatsoever. The empty local change history points outward; nobody followed that pointer.
