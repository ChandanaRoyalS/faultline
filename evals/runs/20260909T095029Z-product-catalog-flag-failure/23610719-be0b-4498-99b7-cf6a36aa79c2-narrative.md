# Unresolved critical alert across seven services, starting at frontend

## What was visible, in order

The page named frontend, loadgenerator and productcatalogservice, with a declared blast radius of seven services and frontend as the origin. Onset is T+0.

First move was frontend's error ratio, since the page pointed there. That produced the most disorienting result of the investigation: frontend's error ratio during the incident window averaged about 1.1 percent against a baseline mean of about 3.7 percent, with a lower peak than baseline. Sample counts were full and continuous, so frontend never stopped serving, and no sustained departure was flagged. The service we were told to start from looked healthier than usual. Note the structural limit: the frontend series is aggregated by service name only, with no peer dimension, so it can neither implicate nor clear any specific call path.

Second move was productcatalogservice, and this is the only positive signal we have. Its error ratio was a hard zero across the whole 32-minute baseline (129 samples, zero variance) and rose during the incident window to a mean near 1.2 percent with a peak around 10 percent. The single detected change point sits about two minutes before the alert; most of the window was still near-clean. Sampling was dense and untruncated, so neither the clean baseline nor the late onset is a coverage artifact. This closes a chronic error floor (baseline is exactly zero) and a total outage or crash loop (roughly 90 percent of calls still succeeded at the worst point), and it strains the idea that this service broke first, since its errors arrive at the very end. Crucially, only the error-ratio series came back: no latency, CPU, memory or restart data for any service.

Third move was traces. Ten sampled traces touching productcatalogservice all shared an identical three-span shape: frontend HTTP GET, frontend gRPC client span for the product lookup, and a productcatalogservice server span that is a leaf in every trace. The handler itself is essentially instantaneous at 0.0 to 0.1 milliseconds, while the frontend's client span for the same call carries 0.7 to 12.7 milliseconds of self-time. Whatever cost exists sits on the client side of that one hop. This rules out a slow handler, a deep multi-hop failure (every trace is two services and three spans), connection-refused before arrival (a server span completes each time), and any downstream dependency of the callee, which has none in these samples. Two caveats: the returned traces cluster into the final seven seconds of the window, leaving the minutes right after the error change point unrepresented, and no status codes or error tags surfaced, so we cannot say these are the failing spans.

## The dead ends, and why they cost time

Every change record examined was an automation apply/revert pair already backed out well before onset. On productcatalogservice, exactly two entries in 24 hours: a traffic-shaping container applying a fixed 300ms egress delay, created and removed within about nine minutes, roughly 3.8 hours before onset, with no deployment, image, config or flag change anywhere in the window. On cartservice, 24 entries forming a repeating self-reverting cycle — a hotfix image tag, a 300ms egress delay, and a Redis address override — each element individually paired with its revert, the last cycle completing about 59 minutes before onset and nothing at or after onset. On adservice, six entries, all edits to the same memory limit, three matched apply-then-revert pairs, the last revert removing the limit about 1.9 hours prior. All attributed to a single automation actor; no human edits. Nothing was left running, and nothing landed adjacent to onset.

Logs returned absolutely nothing for productcatalogservice across the full window. That silence is ours: the selector used a hyphenated label value while the service is spelled as one word. Treat the three hypotheses nominally closed there — a logged panic, an explicit upstream dependency error, any log-visible cause — as untested. Window scoping was not the limiting factor.

One metrics dispatch reported a flat-zero error ratio over six hours with a mildly erroring baseline, i.e. the exact inverse of the finding above. It queried a window a full day before onset. Its "incident" segment is ordinary operation and its "baseline" contains the period we care about, which is why the change point it discusses falls outside its own incident segment. Discard it as incident-window evidence; it is recorded only so a future reader knows which of two contradictory conclusions to drop.

## Where this stands and what to do first

The evidence does not establish a mechanism. Confidence is low and no fix class is proposed. The only defensible statement is that a modest, late error rise appeared on productcatalogservice while frontend errors fell below baseline, and that in the sampled traces the self-time on that hop sits on the caller's side rather than in the callee.

For whoever picks this up, in priority order. Retrieve productcatalogservice request latency for the incident window — no latency series was ever fetched in any dispatch, leaving the single most load-bearing hypothesis unmeasured. Re-run the logs with the correct one-word service label. Then identify what actually fired the alert: three services paged while frontend errors were below baseline, and loadgenerator was never investigated at all.

Also still open: one triage edge was crossed unmeasured, and adservice, cartservice, checkoutservice and loadgenerator received no metrics, logs or traces, so an origin outside productcatalogservice remains live. And query span tags directly to learn whether the spans carry error status and whether any traces exist in the gap between the error change point and the late cluster.
