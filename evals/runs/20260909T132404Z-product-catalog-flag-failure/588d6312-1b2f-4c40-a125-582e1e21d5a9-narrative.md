# Intermittent GetProduct failures in productcatalogservice

## What we saw first

Three alerts landed together: loadgenerator, productcatalogservice, and frontend. Blast radius was reported as seven services and severity critical, which set an expectation of something broad and loud. It was not. The starting point given to the responder was productcatalogservice, and that turned out to be correct, but the shape of the problem was much smaller than the alert set suggested.

The first thing worth knowing about the whole record: one of the seven edges in the radius was never measured, and the frontend alert was a red herring from the start. Read the frontend section before you spend time on it.

## The error signal in productcatalogservice

The cleanest evidence in the entire record is the error-ratio comparison for productcatalogservice. Across a four-hour baseline of 968 samples, the ratio was exactly zero — mean, min, max and standard deviation all zero. That makes the subsequent non-zero behaviour unambiguously new rather than a pre-existing floor, which is a rare luxury.

Errors began around 09:52, roughly three and a half hours before the timestamp the responder was handed, with a second threshold crossing about two minutes before the alert. Mean was around 0.5% with peaks near 12%, and the standard deviation ran several times the mean. That combination — low mean, high peak, high variance, only two threshold crossings in four hours — describes short separated bursts, not a ramp and not a saturating outage. The overwhelming majority of calls kept succeeding.

The important correction this produced: nothing that happened at or immediately before the alert time can explain onset. Onset was hours earlier. Anything found near the alert timestamp can, at best, explain the second burst.

## The one error trace, and what it puts the blame on

The trace sample over the window returned 10 traces and 150 spans, and exactly one span anywhere in that set was in error. That sparsity is itself a finding: a broad high-volume failure would have shown densely.

The erroring span is productcatalogservice's own GetProduct server handler, reached from checkoutservice, and the error propagates upward through checkoutservice/PlaceOrder, frontend's gRPC PlaceOrder, and out to a frontend HTTP POST. The hop attribution names the checkout→productcatalog GetProduct hop as the degrading one.

Two details in that span narrow things sharply. It fails in about 0.8ms with roughly 0.1ms of self time — a fast in-handler rejection, not a timeout. And its only child call, a feature-flag read, completes in about 0.7ms and is not marked in error. So the failure originates in the handler, immediately after reading a flag, with nothing slow or failing beneath it.

Interestingly, the error trace is timestamped about a minute *before* the second burst window, and the two traces sampled inside that burst window are clean, with sub-millisecond server spans. Sampling density, not absence of failure, explains that.

## Dead end: looking for a change to blame

The instinct was to find a deploy or a config push near onset. The change history for productcatalogservice covers the full window and contains exactly two entries, both from platform automation and both about seven and a half hours before onset: a traffic-shaping container attached to the service's network namespace adding a fixed 300ms egress delay, and its removal roughly eight and a half minutes later.

That pair is net-zero — the delay parameter returns to unset — and it was gone long before errors started. It briefly looked promising and it is not the cause. No deploy, no image or tag movement, no config push is recorded anywhere in the window, and the record is empty for the hours directly preceding the alert. Neither entry is operator activity during response.

So the fix class is a config revert, but the record contains no config change to revert. That gap is the central weakness of the conclusion.

## Dead end: productcatalogservice logs

A log query spanning roughly thirty-two minutes around the alert returned nothing at all. Widening the time range under the same selector will not help — the window already brackets the timestamp generously on both sides and is uniformly empty.

The likely explanation is mundane and important: the selector used a hyphenated label value that does not match the service name in scope. So this is almost certainly a label mismatch, not silence from the service. Error returns, panics and restarts remain genuinely unexamined. Re-querying with the correct label is the single highest-value next step for whoever picks this up.

## Dead end: the frontend alert

Frontend alerted, so it got investigated as a possible independent problem or as the place to attribute downstream blame. It was neither.

Frontend's error ratio during the incident window averaged about 0.9%, roughly a seventh of its own preceding baseline of about 6.4%, with no sustained departure flagged. Even the worst moment in the window (~9.2%) sat well below the baseline peak (~33%). The frontend alert reflects propagation of a small number of failed checkout requests, nothing more.

Two cautions the responder should carry forward. First, frontend's baseline is noisy — 6.4% mean, 33% peak, high variance — so it cannot be treated as a quiet reference for anything. Second, the metric is a single service-level aggregate with no per-route or per-dependency breakdown, so it could never have attributed frontend errors to a specific downstream even if there had been an elevation to attribute.

## Dead end: downstream of checkout

Checkoutservice sits directly above the failing hop, so it was worth checking whether the real trouble lived further down. It does not. Every returned checkout log line is info severity; none names the product-catalog RPCs, none carries a gRPC status code. Through the last minute of the window checkout was still completing whole order flows — place order, payment authorization, confirmation email, successful queue write with advancing offsets.

Payment, email and the order queue are all clean, which rules them out as the source. Two place-order entries near the very end lack their usual follow-on trio, but with only two instances that reads as orders in flight at cutoff, not a pattern.

Coverage caveat: the result was truncated to the oldest eight and newest thirty-two lines, so most of the minutes leading into the alert were never returned. The ruling-out is solid for the dense final minute and weaker before it.

## Where this landed

productcatalogservice is failing a small, intermittent fraction of its own GetProduct calls. The failure is in the handler itself: fast rejection, negligible self time, no latency or saturation anywhere in the path, and it occurs immediately after a feature-flag read whose own call is fast and healthy. Downstream services are not the source, and the frontend alert is propagation.

The inferred mechanism is a flag-gated failure mode enabled inside productcatalogservice — the flag value being what makes the request fail. Hence config revert as the fix class.

Confidence is low, and deliberately so. The reasoning rests on trace shape alone.

## Still open

Three things a later reader should not assume were done.

The feature flag state was never actually queried. The flag-gated mechanism is inference from the shape of one span and its one child. It is unconfirmed.

The service's own logs were never really read, because of the probable label mismatch described above. Errors, panics and restarts are all still unknown. Fix the selector and re-query first.

And nothing explains what started the errors around 09:52. With a single error trace in hand, it is impossible to say whether the failures are scoped by product id, by calling service, or fire at a fixed probability. Notably, only the checkout-driven GetProduct path carried an error in the sample while the frontend product-page GetProduct calls and the recommendation-driven ListProducts calls were all clean — that may be a real scoping clue or pure sampling luck, and one trace cannot distinguish the two.
