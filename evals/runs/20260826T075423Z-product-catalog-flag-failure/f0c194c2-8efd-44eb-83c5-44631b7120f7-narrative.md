# Frontend 5xx traced to partial gRPC INTERNAL responses from productcatalogservice

## What paged, and in what order

Three alerts arrived effectively together: loadgenerator, frontend, and productcatalogservice. Triage put the blast radius at seven services with the entry point at frontend, and flagged one edge in the call graph as unmeasured. Treat the moment the pages fired as T+0 for everything below.

The first thing worth knowing is that the pages were late relative to the symptom. Frontend was already logging the same error class roughly three minutes before anything paged (T-3m). That gap is unexplained and was never closed. If you are reading this during a repeat, do not anchor your search window on the alert time — go back at least fifteen minutes further than feels necessary.

## First useful signal: the frontend logs

Frontend was the loudest service and the natural place to start, and it was also the first dead end — in the productive sense. Its logs carried repeated gRPC client errors with status code 13 (INTERNAL). Every stack frame sat in the frontend's gRPC client status-receive path, not in frontend business logic. That placement is the whole finding: frontend was not generating these errors, it was being handed them and dutifully surfacing them to the user.

The detail text attached to the status named ProductCatalogService as the callee and attributed the failure to an enabled 'fail' feature flag on that service. Occurrences ran from T-3m through at least T+2m30s, so this was sustained, not a blip.

Three hypotheses died here. It was not a network or transport problem — code 13 with an application-level detail message means the request arrived and the callee answered; a transport break would have shown as 14 UNAVAILABLE or 4 DEADLINE_EXCEEDED. It was not auth, quota, or bad arguments — none of the corresponding status codes appeared. And it was not self-resolving; identical errors were still present at the end of the queried window.

## Confirming the callee was up but wrong

With the frontend logs pointing downstream, the next question was whether productcatalogservice was actually broken or merely being blamed. Its error ratio (error-status calls over total calls, two-minute rate) was non-zero across the entire window, thirty-one continuous sample points, ranging from roughly 0.6% at the low end to about 17% at the peak.

That shape rules out several plausible stories at once. It is not a healthy service being scapegoated — 17% is nowhere near a clean baseline. It is not a hard-down or crash-looping service — the error fraction never approaches 1.0 and the majority of requests kept succeeding. It is not a metrics-pipeline gap masquerading as a failure — the series is continuous with no dropouts. And it is not a steady background error rate from a persistent bad client or a fixed misconfiguration — a roughly thirty-fold swing inside fifteen minutes is not what a constant-rate cause produces.

A partial, variable error rate on a service that is otherwise up and serving is the signature that matched the frontend's log detail: the service is deliberately returning failures for a fraction of calls.

## The dead end: change history

This is the part worth reading twice, because two separate passes went down the same hole.

The obvious next move was to find who enabled the flag and when. Both change-history queries against productcatalogservice returned nothing at all — no deploys, no config edits, no flag flips, no rollouts. Empty.

From that emptiness a few things genuinely follow. There was no productcatalogservice deploy or rollout around onset, so there was nothing to pause and nothing to roll back as a first move. There was no service-attributed config push in the bracket either, since config and flag events share the same change-log source that came back empty.

But the coverage was narrower than the question. Both queries spanned only T-10m to T+5m — fifteen minutes — when the ask was for the full hour before onset. Both were scoped to productcatalogservice alone. Neither touched a shared or global feature-flag store, and neither looked at frontend's own history. So the correct reading of the empty result is not "no change happened" but "the change is not recorded here, in this window, against this service." Given the flag was already producing errors at T-3m and possibly earlier, the enabling event most likely landed before the window opened, or lives in a flag store nobody searched.

If you hit this again: widen the window first, then search the flag store, not the service's change log.

## Conclusion and fix class

productcatalogservice was returning gRPC INTERNAL to a fraction of incoming calls because a 'fail' feature flag was enabled on it. Frontend was the visible victim and the alerting surface; loadgenerator paged as the edge-most observer of the same symptom. Fix class is config revert — disable the flag. Confidence is medium, and the reason it is only medium is entirely in the next section.

## Open questions and what was never looked at

Carry these forward; none were closed.

The flag flip is unattributed. No actor, no timestamp. The change queries covered fifteen minutes and one service, and never searched a shared or global flag store.

The flag state was never read directly — not from productcatalogservice and not from a flag service. The entire diagnosis rests on detail text in the frontend's log lines. That text is specific and consistent, but it is a single source and it is the callee describing itself to a caller.

The three-minute gap between first observed error (T-3m) and the pages (T+0) is unexplained. It may simply be alert evaluation delay, or it may mean the flag was enabled meaningfully earlier and low-rate errors sat under threshold.

The roughly thirty-fold swing in error ratio was never explained. A percentage-based error return, load correlation, and a partially rolled-out flag are all consistent with the observed curve and were not distinguished from one another.

Blast radius named seven services; only two were investigated. Four others were never examined, and it is unknown whether any of them also carry the flag.

One edge in the call graph was marked unmeasured and was never identified. A contributing path between frontend and productcatalogservice, or beyond it, remains unobserved.

Finally, frontend's own change history was never queried. The log stack places the origin downstream, which is strong, but a coincident frontend change cannot be formally excluded.
