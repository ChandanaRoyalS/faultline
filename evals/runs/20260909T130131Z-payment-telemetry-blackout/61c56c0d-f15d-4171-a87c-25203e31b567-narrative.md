# paymentservice paged while its telemetry went nowhere

## What the responder saw first

The page named paymentservice and carried critical severity, with a declared blast radius of three services. Two of the edges crossed during the walk were unmeasured, so from the first minute there was no instrumented path linking the alerting service to its neighbours.

The first thing that stood out at T+0 was how little there was to look at. The alert pointed at payments, but every place a responder would normally look for corroboration came back thin or empty. That emptiness turned out to be the story rather than an obstacle to it, but it took most of the investigation to establish that.

## The metric that never existed

The obvious first move was the error-ratio template for paymentservice. It returned nothing — no samples in the incident window and none in the half-hour baseline before it. Widening the baseline out to roughly two hours before the page produced the same nothing. That second query is the important one: because the earlier window is equally empty, the gap cannot be something the incident did. There is no before-and-after to point at.

This is also the first dead end worth preserving. The tooling summarised the wider comparison as "no sustained departure from the baseline," which reads as reassurance. It is not. The comparison was computed over zero samples on both sides and says nothing at all about error rate. A responder skimming that line would conclude payments was fine on the metrics and move on.

The span-derived series behind the query, calls_total, was only ever tried under one selector — service_name="paymentservice". That selector was already flagged as suspect and it never returned a row across four hours. Nothing was queried for request rate, latency percentiles, or saturation, so this branch carries no information about those at all.

## Traces: absent, and absent for longer than the incident

Traces were the next stop, on the theory that a span breakdown would localise added latency on the charge path. The query covering the full two-hour span returned zero traces for paymentservice — not sparse, not sampled, zero.

The attractive hypothesis here was that something at roughly T-7m broke the trace pipeline and that the trace gap was the incident's own footprint. That was ruled out on its own evidence: spans are equally missing across more than a hundred minutes of pre-onset baseline. Whatever keeps paymentservice spans out of this backend was already in place well before the page. That leaves open a longer-standing instrumentation gap, or a service-name or tenant mismatch in the trace store, and neither was tested.

Practical consequence: span-level localisation was unavailable for the whole investigation. Any question of the form "which call on the charge path got slow" had to be abandoned and re-asked against logs.

## Logs say the business path is healthy

Payment logs are reachable, but under the hyphenated label value payment-service rather than paymentservice — a small snag that costs a query if you don't hit it early. Once found, the content was uniformly informational: charge requests each followed within about a millisecond by a completed transaction carrying a transaction id and card type, running continuously to 13:09:51. No errors, no warnings, no stack traces, nothing naming a downstream dependency or a credential.

That kills several candidate stories outright. Payments was not down or crash-looping — it was serving through the end of the window. Charges were not being accepted and left hanging — every request in the returned data has its completion. And the service is certainly shipping logs; the result was truncated for volume.

Checkout told the same story from the other side: complete order flows (PlaceOrder, payment success with a transaction id, confirmation email, successful queue write), all info level, with queue offsets advancing steadily from roughly 22910 to 22917 between 13:08:47 and 13:09:51 and payment confirmations landing 14–20 ms after each order entry. No timeouts, no deadline-exceeded, nothing naming payments as a culprit. Checkout was healthy at window close.

One detail to hold loosely: the reporting host identifier differs between the oldest log block and the newest while the process id stays the same, which is consistent with the instance having been replaced somewhere in the middle of the window. Nothing was built on this.

## The change history, and why the obvious read is wrong twice

The change log for paymentservice across the window contains exactly one class of event: repeated toggling of a single environment variable, the OTLP traces endpoint, set to a loopback address. Seven such events, all attributed to the same platform-automation actor. The one closest to onset landed roughly six minutes before the page and set the variable where it had previously been unset.

That single-variable surface let a lot go: no deploy or image rollout appears anywhere in the window, no flag flips, no individual human operator, and no simultaneous drift across unrelated parameters. Those are all cheap eliminations and worth recording as such.

The trap is treating the pre-onset change as self-evidently causal. The identical change had already been applied three times earlier in the same window without a page each time, and each of those earlier applications was reverted within about ten to fifteen minutes. The pre-onset one has no matching revert. So the change alone is not sufficient — what distinguishes this occurrence is that it stuck. The set-and-revert cycle recurs on a rough multi-hour cadence, which reads as an automated reconciliation loop rather than a one-off action.

## Where it landed

The reading that survives: the exporter endpoint was pointed at a loopback port where no collector listens, and this time the value was never put back. paymentservice spans therefore go nowhere, Tempo holds nothing for the service, and the span-derived calls_total series the error-ratio alert query depends on is empty. Meanwhile the business path is demonstrably fine — charges complete sub-millisecond, checkout completes orders end to end with fast payment confirmations. The wrong endpoint value is itself the failing thing: it silently discards the service's telemetry, and it is that discard the alert is reacting to.

Confidence is medium, and the fix class is a config revert of the endpoint variable — plus a look at whatever loop keeps reapplying it.

## Open threads for the next responder

Three things this record cannot close.

First, the signal that actually fired the page was never retrieved. The link between empty telemetry and the alert is inferred, not observed. Pull the alert rule and its evaluation history before trusting the conclusion.

Second, both log queries were truncated to the oldest few and newest few lines, which omitted roughly 12:38 through 13:07:57 — the interval containing the alert timestamp itself. A short degradation at onset that healed on its own is not excluded by anything here. Re-run the log queries narrowed to that gap.

Third, the trace absence covers more than a hundred minutes before the change that supposedly caused it, and the variable was reportedly reverted well earlier in the day. That is a real inconsistency. A longer-standing instrumentation gap, or a label or tenant mismatch in the trace store, may explain the missing spans instead. calls_total was never tried under any selector other than the one already known to be wrong; try the app or job label, or the hyphenated name, before accepting the endpoint value as the whole cause.
