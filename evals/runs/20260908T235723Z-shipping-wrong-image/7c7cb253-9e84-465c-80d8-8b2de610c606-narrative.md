# Checkout order completions stalling with intermittent errors

## What the responder saw first

The page arrived for checkoutservice with a critical blast radius spanning eleven services. Five more services alerted about three minutes behind it: accountingservice, emailservice, frauddetectionservice, quoteservice and shippingservice. Because checkoutservice was both the seed and the loudest signal, the first instinct was to treat it as the broken component. That instinct turned out to be wrong, and unpicking it consumed most of the investigation.

The first hard measurement was the checkoutservice error ratio against the immediately preceding baseline. The baseline period was essentially clean (mean around three ten-thousandths), while the incident window ran around 12% with peaks near 30%. Onset landed at T+0 (roughly eight minutes into the queried window), with a second threshold crossing at about T+20m. Minimum stayed at zero throughout and the spread was as large as the mean, so this was intermittent partial failure, not a hard outage.

## The log read that reframed the incident

Checkoutservice logs across a wider window were the turning point. Early in the window each request produced a full lifecycle: order placed, payment confirmed with a transaction id, confirmation email sent, and a downstream message write with a monotonically increasing offset. In the last few minutes of the window only the order-placement lines remain. Orders are still being accepted at a steady cadence of a few seconds, so the process is alive and serving, but the payment, email and message-write completion lines stop appearing.

Equally important is what is absent: not a single error- or warn-level line anywhere in the returned range. No status codes, no peer names, no hostnames, no config values. That rules out the comfortable outcome where the service names its own culprit. It also rules out the loud failure modes — no panic, no boot-time config rejection, no restart or shutdown banner. What is left is silent non-completion of in-flight calls, which reads as a wait that eventually times out rather than a rejection. On that reading, checkoutservice is the service that noticed the problem, not the service that has it.

## Dead end one: a change to checkoutservice

The change log was queried for checkoutservice across the full day-long window and came back genuinely empty — not truncated, not partial. No deploys, no config edits, no flag flips, no environment or credential updates, no reverts. Every variant of "someone shipped something to checkout" is off the table for the queried interval. This is a clean negative and worth keeping: it is the cheapest way to stop a responder re-running the same query.

## Dead end two: the neighbouring change records

Three neighbours were checked against the change log, and all three produced activity that looked promising at a glance and dissolved on inspection.

cartservice had twenty entries, all from the same platform automation actor, arranged in matched apply/revert pairs across the day: an image pinned to a hotfix tag and reverted, a Redis address pointed at a non-default port and reverted, and a traffic-shaping sidecar attached to the service network namespace and later removed. Every pair closed before onset; the nearest event is a revert roughly 2.3 hours prior. So at onset cartservice was back at baseline image, environment and namespace state.

productcatalogservice had exactly two entries — a shaping sidecar attached and then detached about eight and a half minutes later — both around 15.8 hours before onset. No deploy or application config record at all.

paymentservice had four entries, all touching one environment variable (the traces exporter endpoint), as two identical set/revert pairs. The nearer pair closed about 1.4 hours before onset with the variable back to unset, and the earlier identical pair ran roughly 17 hours earlier with no incident following it. Two arguments against causation there: no net configuration difference persisted into onset, and the same manipulation had previously been harmless.

All of this activity is automated and self-reverting. None of it was standing at onset. The temptation to pin the incident on the most recent change record is exactly the trap this section exists to close.

## Where the investigation stopped

The change-query budget was exhausted at four of four before any per-peer evidence was gathered. The metric that established onset aggregated by service name only — no dimension for outbound peer or RPC method, and neither request rate nor latency series were pulled. No downstream service logs were read. No infrastructure or resource metrics were collected.

So the conclusion is provisional and low confidence: a dependency in the checkout fan-out — the payment, email and message-write path — became slow, and checkoutservice failed on timeout. The named suspect is paymentservice, chosen only because payment confirmation is the first step in the observed sequence to stop completing. That is an ordering argument, not a measurement. No fix class is proposed because nothing was measured well enough to justify one.

## Open questions for the next responder

First and most valuable: which downstream peer actually slowed. A latency and error breakdown by outbound peer and method from checkoutservice would settle in one query what the whole record above only circles.

Second: the message-broker write path. The missing offset lines are as consistent with a stalled broker write as with a slow payment call, and the payment-first ordering could simply reflect log sequence rather than the stall point.

Third: why the five downstream services alerted about three minutes behind checkoutservice. Are they fellow victims of one slow shared dependency, or is one of them the origin? No change records were pulled for emailservice, shippingservice, quoteservice, frauddetectionservice, accountingservice or the broker.

Fourth: whether something below the application layer — broker, Redis, node saturation — underlies all of it. No resource evidence exists either way.
