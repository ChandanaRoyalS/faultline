## What was visible first

Three alerts: frontend, adservice, loadgenerator. Blast radius was scored at seven services with one edge that carries no telemetry at all. The first thing actually measured was frontend's error ratio, and it was unambiguous: exactly zero across every one of 69 samples over the 35 minutes before onset, then a window mean near 1.9% with an 8.4% peak. Two distinct threshold crossings, roughly four minutes apart, with a standard deviation larger than the mean — spiky, not a step. That shape mattered later. It also killed two early framings on the spot: there was no slow-burning pre-existing error background (the baseline is a hard zero, not a low hum), and frontend was not down (over 90% of calls still succeeded, so no critical dependency had gone hard-off).

## The second dead end: adservice's own metrics

Two separate attempts to get a signal out of adservice's call metrics returned nothing — zero samples in the incident window. This is the single most misleading result in the record, because an empty error-ratio series reads at a glance like either a healthy flat line or a dead pod. It is neither. The identical query is also empty across the whole pre-onset baseline, when the service was demonstrably serving traffic. That means the emptiness is a collection or labelling gap for this service that predates the incident entirely, and null results from this metric family carry no information about service state in either direction. Do not read the gap as a crash signature, and do not read the 'no departure from baseline' verdict as evidence of health. The second attempt was aimed at memory, restart-count and kill telemetry and came back with the same error-ratio family instead — so that question was never actually asked of the data.

## What the logs actually showed

The ad-service log stream is where the picture resolved. From roughly T+17s onward the same three lines repeat over and over — the tool-options pickup, a class-sharing VM warning, and the tracing agent version banner — initially at irregular short intervals of eight to thirty seconds, then settling into a near-constant sixty-second cadence that runs to the end of the window. Normal ad request handling with trace ids appears only in the early part of the window, well before onset, and never again. The process is being created and destroyed on a cycle and never reaches the request-serving stage.

Several plausible readings die here. There is no application exception, no ERROR line, no stack trace anywhere after onset, so nothing names a failing downstream component. There is no self-reported fatal JVM condition either — no out-of-memory message, no heap dump notice, no startup misconfiguration complaint. Each lifecycle emits its benign boilerplate and then simply stops, which is the signature of the process being terminated from outside rather than dying of something it could describe. It was not hung: a wedged process would not keep re-emitting startup lines. It was not serving degraded responses: with no request-received lines at all, downstream ad failures come from unavailability, not bad answers. And the tracing agent was not the culprit — its banner is emitted successfully on every single restart, so instrumentation completes before the process dies.

## The change that explains it

The change record for adservice holds nine entries, all of one kind: the ad-service container memory limit being lowered to 256m and restored, over and over, every one of them attributed to platform automation. The cadence is roughly four-hourly across the day — four complete lower-then-restore pairs, each restored within about ten minutes. The fifth lowering, at T+0, was never restored. That constrained limit was in effect at onset and stayed in effect.

This also cleared the usual suspects. No deploy, no image or version rollout — every entry is a limit edit. No feature flag flip or application config change appears for adservice or its shared config. No human actor appears anywhere; this was not an unreviewed manual edit. And the 256m value itself is not novel or exotic — it had been applied four times already that day without incident. What differs this cycle is only the missing restore.

## Working conclusion

With 256m available the JVM could not finish starting inside its allocation, so the container was killed from outside on each attempt, producing the sixty-second loop. Because adservice never reached the request-serving stage, frontend's ad calls failed — but only for the fraction of requests that landed while the dependency was absent, which is exactly the spiky 1.9%-mean / 8.4%-peak pattern observed rather than a sustained step. Fix class is a config revert: restore the memory limit. Confidence is medium, and the reason it is not higher is set out below.

## Still open — read this before you trust the conclusion

The termination mechanism is inferred, not observed. No memory working-set, kill-event, or restart-count telemetry for ad-service was ever retrieved; the chain rests on the silent-termination log pattern plus the limit change. Get that telemetry next time and the confidence question closes.

Why the automation's restore step did not fire on this cycle, after firing four times the same day, is unexplained. The automation itself may be stalled or broken, and reverting the limit by hand does not address that.

The adservice call-metric gap predates the incident and was never investigated. It is a standing monitoring hole that made this incident harder than it needed to be.

Frontend's error ratio was only ever aggregated by service name — no per-route, per-dependency, or latency dimension. The ad call path is therefore inferred as the failing path, not measured as such.

Triage counted seven affected services and one edge with no measurement across it. Only frontend and adservice were examined. A second concurrent contributor sitting in the other four services, or across the unmeasured edge, cannot be excluded.

The loadgenerator alert was never investigated and is simply assumed to be downstream of frontend.
