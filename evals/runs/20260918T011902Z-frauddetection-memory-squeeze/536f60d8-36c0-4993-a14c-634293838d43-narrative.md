# frauddetection-service restart loop under a lowered memory ceiling

## What the responder saw first

The page named exactly one service: frauddetectionservice. Severity critical, blast radius one service, and no unmeasured edges crossed on the way in. At the moment of the alert (call it T+0) the only thing visible from the service itself was a short burst of Java startup lines — the tool-options pickup, a class-sharing warning from the JVM, and the OpenTelemetry agent banner — and nothing else. No exception, no stack trace, no error-level line anywhere. A responder reading only the alert would reasonably expect an application error; there was none to find.

## The shape of the failure

Widening the log window turned that single startup burst into a pattern. The identical three-line startup sequence appears seven times over the eight minutes spanning roughly T-5m to T+1m, spaced about 62 seconds apart, with a tighter cluster of restarts (five to fifteen seconds apart) just before the cadence settled — that cluster begins around T-6m. Between iterations there is no shutdown message, no graceful-stop line, nothing indicating the process chose to exit. The process is being recreated from outside.

Equally important is what stops appearing. Earlier in the same window, up to about T-29m, the service was doing its normal work: consuming order records with an incrementing counter. After the loop begins, not one consumption line appears in any iteration. The process dies before or just after reaching working state, so it never gets far enough to do anything useful. That rules out the comfortable reading that this was a degraded or stalled consumer inside a long-lived process, and it rules out the reading that the service had been silently broken all window — it was demonstrably healthy at the start.

## The change that lines up

The change log for this service across the previous 24 hours contains exactly one kind of entry, repeated: an automated actor named platform-automation lowering the service's memory limit to 200m and later restoring it. The most recent lowering landed about six minutes before the alert — roughly T-6m — and had not been restored when the incident window opened. So the service entered the loop running under the tightened ceiling.

What that history does *not* contain is worth recording, because each absence closed a line of inquiry a responder would otherwise chase: no deploy or version release, no feature-flag flip, no library or dependency bump, and no human actor at all. Eleven entries, one automated origin.

## Checking the blast radius

Before accepting frauddetectionservice as the failing service rather than merely the service that noticed, we read checkoutservice's logs over the same window. They are uniformly info-severity and follow an unbroken four-line rhythm per order: placement, payment success, confirmation email, successful queue write. Orders were still completing at T+1m, with queue-write offsets incrementing by exactly one per order across the tail of the window — no gaps, no stalls, no retry or timeout wording, no restart or shutdown lines. Behaviour at the start of the window is indistinguishable from behaviour at the end.

The caveat: checkout's logs never name the fraud-check dependency at all. So this evidence establishes that the synchronous order path kept working; it says nothing about the asynchronous consumer that was down. Containment is real but narrower than it first looks.

## Dead ends

Two dead ends cost real time and should not be repeated.

First, the error-ratio metric. The standard span-based error-ratio query for this service returned zero samples — not zeros, *no samples* — across the incident window, across an equal-length window immediately before it, and across a six-hour baseline a day earlier. The tempting inference is that the restart loop knocked the pod out of service and killed the metric. That inference is wrong: the baseline six hours before onset is equally empty, so whatever suppresses these series predates the incident entirely. An empty result is also not a healthy result; this signal can neither confirm nor deny a spike. Treat this metric path as unusable for this service.

Second, the assumption that this was a one-off or first-time change. It was not. The same lowering had run five times in the preceding twenty hours, four of them restored after about eleven minutes each, with no incident recorded. Whatever distinguishes this instance is not the existence of the change.

Third, a smaller one: the restart interval does not fit exponential crash-loop backoff. Intervals from T-5m onward are near-constant at about 62 seconds rather than lengthening.

## Conclusion and confidence

The reading we settled on: a process that was working normally began dying abruptly and externally about six minutes after its memory ceiling was cut to 200m, and never again reached working state. The cause is the limit edit; the failing mechanism is the process needing more memory than it is now allowed and being terminated for it. Fix class is a revert of that configuration.

Confidence is medium, deliberately. The kill mechanism is inferred from the log signature, not observed.

## Open questions for the next responder

No container-level evidence was ever gathered. Working-set bytes against the 200m ceiling, restart counts, and the last-terminated reason were never queried. Query those first; they would settle in one step what we inferred in several.

The near-constant 62-second cadence argues against a pure memory kill, which usually varies with allocation rate. A fixed-period health check killing the container — plausibly because the JVM cannot warm up inside its threshold under 200m — fits the timing better and remains untested.

Why this instance turned into an incident when four prior cycles did not is unexplained. Candidates: traffic level, heap growth, or simply that this one was not restored on schedule. Relatedly, whether the automation will restore the limit on its own cadence or has stalled mid-cycle was never established — worth checking before assuming a manual revert is required.

The empty metric series predates the incident and is unexplained. It may be concealing earlier occurrences of exactly this failure.

Finally, downstream consequence is unmeasured. Order records were accumulating unprocessed for the whole loop, and nothing we looked at tells us how large that backlog grew or when it would have surfaced to a customer.
