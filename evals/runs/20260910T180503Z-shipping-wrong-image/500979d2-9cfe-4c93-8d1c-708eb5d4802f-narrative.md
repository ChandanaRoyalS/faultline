# Checkout requests stall after order entry; downstream payment hop suspected

## What was visible first

The page came from two places at once: checkoutservice and the load generator. Severity critical, ten services in the blast radius, and four edges in the call graph that no one had ever measured. The first thing worth knowing is that the alert time was not the onset time. The span error ratio for checkoutservice had already crossed its baseline threshold roughly twenty-three minutes before the page (call the page T+0; the first crossing sits at T-23m), with a second crossing about ninety seconds before the page fired. Baseline error ratio was around 1.2%; the incident window averaged about 11.7% with a peak near 29%. That peak matters as much as the mean: errors were partial and intermittent the whole way through, dipping back to zero between bursts. Nothing about this looked like a hard outage.

## Is checkoutservice itself broken?

No. Two separate passes over the change log covering the full 24 hours around the incident came back completely empty for checkoutservice — no release, no config edit, no flag flip, no repointing at a different downstream address. There was nothing to roll back, which is worth stating plainly because 'roll back the last change' is the reflex and here it would have burned time. Note the honest caveat on the first pass: its window started at the alert timestamp and ran forward, so it did not actually cover the hours before onset. The second pass covered the preceding day and was also empty, which is what closes the question.

## The logs told the story the metrics could not

The service log was the highest-value read of the whole investigation, and it is silent in an interesting way: not one error or warn line, not one stack trace, only info. Early in the window, each order request is followed by a tidy sequence — payment confirmation with a transaction id, then the confirmation email, then a successful message write with an incrementing offset. From roughly T-1m onward, only the order-entry line appears. Over and over. None of the follow-up lines. Requests keep arriving at a normal cadence of one to twenty seconds right through the end of the window, so the process is alive, serving, and logging. What changed is what happens after the request lands: it begins and then hangs before the first downstream step that logs a result. A fraction of those eventually surface as errors, which is exactly the shape a timeout against a slow dependency makes. Two tempting readings die here. The payment step is not rejecting or declining anything — there are no payment failure lines at all, it simply never logs. And this is not confined to one currency path; the stalled requests carry more than one currency value.

## Dead ends, and why they were worth walking

productcatalogservice was cleared properly. Its error ratio is identically zero across 133 samples in the incident window, and — the detail that makes the clearance trustworthy — the preceding baseline window carried a small nonzero ratio, peaking around 12%. So the collector was working and reporting real data; the flat line is a measurement, not a gap. It cannot be the source of errors seen elsewhere.

cartservice and shippingservice are a different matter and were nearly mis-read. Both returned zero samples. That is not a flat line and it is not health. Critically, their baseline windows were equally empty, so the gap predates onset — this is not telemetry dropping out as a symptom. It is most likely that the series does not exist under the queried labels for these services at all. Both remain neither implicated nor cleared. Do not let a future record convert these empties into exonerations.

The paymentservice change log looked promising and turned out not to matter. Eight entries in 24 hours, all from the same platform automation actor, all the same telemetry-endpoint environment variable being set and then reverted. The pair nearest onset was set around T-82m and reverted around T-71m, so the variable was back to its prior state roughly 48 minutes before anything moved. The same set/revert cycle repeats four times across the day. Net configuration state at onset was identical to state at the start of the window. No release, no flag, no shared-config propagation, no human hand.

## Where the evidence points

The payment call is the first downstream hop whose completion checkoutservice logs, and it is precisely that line which disappears at onset. Combined with a live, traffic-accepting caller, partial intermittent errors, and no configuration difference on either side, the most probable failing element is paymentservice becoming slow enough that checkout requests block on it and a fraction time out. This is a latency problem in the dependency, not a wrong value in a config file. Confidence is low, and it should be read as low: the conclusion rests entirely on inference from the caller's logs. Suggested first action is a restart of the suspected dependency, which is cheap and reversible given nothing was changed to revert.

## What was never measured

The largest hole: paymentservice, the named suspect, was never measured. No latency query, no error ratio, no saturation, no log read — only its change history was pulled. The conclusion names it without ever having looked at it directly.

Second: no latency data was returned for any service in this incident. Every metrics call evaluated the error-ratio template only, which means the one signal that would most directly confirm a slow-dependency story was never collected. For an incident whose signature is requests hanging, that is the gap to close first next time.

Third: emailservice and the message-broker hop — both of which stopped logging alongside payment — were never queried at all. And cartservice and shippingservice have no samples in either window, so two more edges stay dark. Four unmeasured edges were crossed to reach this conclusion; that is the honest measure of how much weight it can bear.
