# Two Kafka consumers go silent together; broker never examined

## What was visible first

The page came in for fraud-detection, with accounting, payment and email alerting within about a minute of each other roughly six minutes after the quiet began. The responder's starting point was therefore fraud-detection, and the first question asked was the obvious one: what is fraud-detection doing wrong? That framing cost time. Nothing in the record ever showed fraud-detection doing anything wrong — it showed it doing nothing at all.

## The log read that defined the incident (T+0)

Pulling fraud-detection's logs over the half hour around onset returned a single repeated line: the Kafka consumer acknowledging records, with a running total. The count advanced normally, about eight records a minute, from the start of the window to a final entry at T+0 with total 6514. After that, nothing — roughly nine and a half minutes of total silence covering the entire alert period. No error, no exception, no retry, no consumer-group rebalance, no shutdown message, no gradual widening of the gaps between lines. Steady, then stop. That shape matters: a consumer falling behind leaves a taper; a consumer crashing usually leaves a lifecycle line. This left neither.

## The second consumer (T+0, same second)

Accounting's logs were read next and told the same story with different words: routine order-processing records at normal spacing right up to T+0, then complete silence to the end of the window. The result had been truncated in the middle but preserved the newest lines, so the trailing emptiness was genuine rather than an artifact — worth confirming, because a truncation artifact was the first alternative considered and discarded. Two independently deployed services, no shared code path for logging, no shared deploy, stopping in the same second with no complaint from either. That is the moment the investigation should have turned outward to what they share — the broker they fetch from — rather than continuing to interrogate fraud-detection.

## Dead end: the error-ratio chase

Considerable effort went into fraud-detection's error ratio, and it produced nothing usable. A comparison against the immediately preceding half hour showed the mean rising from about 0.15 to about 0.27 — nominally 1.8x — but with enormous dispersion and values spanning zero to one, and the comparison itself reported no sustained departure from baseline. Crucially, the baseline period already contained intervals that were entirely errors, so errors were not new. A second pass against a six-hour baseline looked more dramatic, roughly four times baseline, but its three detected change points all landed hours later, between roughly 01:23 and 02:46 the following morning, nowhere near onset. Worse, that query window started at the alert timestamp and ran forward, so it barely overlapped the period of interest at all. The honest reading: the error signal is noise over sparse samples and does not isolate fraud-detection as anything but a victim.

## Dead end: looking for a change that was not there

Three change-history queries were run — twice against fraud-detection, once against flagd — and all three came back cleanly empty. The queries executed fine and the services are tracked, so the emptiness is a real answer rather than a broken source. But every one of those windows began at the alert timestamp and extended about twenty-four hours forward. They rule out a deploy, config edit, flag flip, or quiet remediation during and after onset. They say nothing whatsoever about the hours before onset, which were never queried for any service. A responder reading this later should treat 'no change found' here as 'no change found after the fact'.

## Where it landed

The working conclusion is that the two consumers did not fail; the broker they both fetch from stopped serving while its socket still accepted connections. Fetches hang, clients block, and a blocked client writes no log line — which is exactly why neither service logged a connection error. The downstream alerts on payment and email about six minutes later fit callers timing out behind the stalled path. Fix class is a restart of the shared component. Confidence is low, and deliberately so: this is the best explanation of the silence, not an observation of its mechanism.

## What was never checked

The broker itself was never queried. Its logs, resource metrics, restart counters and change history are entirely unexamined, so its role is inferred from the shape of two consumers' silence and nothing more. Payment and email alerted but were never opened. And nothing in the evidence distinguishes a genuine processing stop from a log-shipping failure that happened to affect both consumers — the absence of lines is consistent with both. The next responder should start where this one finished: query the shared broker, and query the hours before onset, not after.
