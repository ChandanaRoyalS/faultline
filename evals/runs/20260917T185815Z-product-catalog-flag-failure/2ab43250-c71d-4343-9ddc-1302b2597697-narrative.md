# Selective GetProduct failures in productcatalogservice traced to a flag-conditioned error path

## What was visible first

Three alerts arrived together: loadgenerator, productcatalogservice, and frontend. Blast radius was assessed at seven services, severity critical, with productcatalogservice named as the seed. The stated onset was 19:01:45. From the chair, that was the whole picture at the start: a critical-looking spread of alerts pointing at a catalog service in the middle of the shopping path, and no obvious story about why.

It is worth saying up front what the shape of this incident turned out to be: errors, not slowness. Nothing anywhere in the request path was slow in absolute terms. Root spans ran between roughly 1.4ms and 11.9ms. Anyone arriving expecting a latency blowout will spend time looking for one and not find it.

## The metrics detour (T+0 to roughly T+10m)

The first instinct was to confirm the seed service was actually failing more than usual. Two error-ratio comparisons were run against productcatalogservice, one over a six-hour pre-window and one over a longer stretch. Both came back essentially flat: an incident-window mean around 10% against a baseline near 8.5%, about 1.18x, with comparable variance, and in the second comparison the baseline peak actually exceeded the incident peak. Two threshold crossings were flagged but neither carried a level shift in the mean, and similar excursions existed at baseline.

Read at face value, this evidence argued that productcatalogservice was *not* the origin, that there was no crash-loop or dependency outage, and that no onset marker existed at all. That conclusion was wrong, and understanding why it was wrong is the most useful thing in this record. The service carries a steady-state error floor of roughly 8-10% of calls under normal operation. A failure that affects only a selected subset of requests on one endpoint disappears into that floor. Aggregate error ratio was simply the wrong instrument for this failure mode. If you are handed a similar alert, do not let a flat error ratio talk you out of looking at traces.

The same queries also quietly failed to answer most of what was asked of them. No latency percentiles, no CPU or memory saturation, no container restart counts were ever returned. Those series were never seen, in either attempt.

## The logs dead end

A log query was issued against the hyphenated label value "product-catalog-service" over a roughly 32-minute window ending after the onset timestamp. It returned zero lines. The window was not the problem — it already straddled the time of interest with margin. The label value was: the service under investigation is named without hyphens, so the selector almost certainly matched no stream at all.

The practical consequence is that logs contributed nothing to this investigation in either direction. There is no evidence of a panic or stack trace inside the service, and no evidence naming a downstream peer, because nothing was retrieved. Anyone picking this up should re-issue the query after listing label values, rather than treating the empty result as a finding.

## Change history: an empty answer for the seed, a noisy one next door

A change-history query for productcatalogservice covering roughly the preceding 24 hours returned no entries whatsoever — no releases, no config or environment edits, no flag-state changes recorded against the service. That absence is scoped strictly to the seed service, zero hops, so it says nothing about its dependencies or about shared platform components. It does mean there is no service-owned change to roll back.

The more distracting result came from cartservice. Its change log held 26 entries in the same window, all attributed to platform automation, forming four to five repeating apply-then-revert cycles: a hotfix image tag set and reverted, a traffic-shaping sidecar attached to the cart-service network namespace with a fixed egress delay and then removed, and a REDIS_ADDR override pointing at a non-default redis-cart port, set and reverted. This looked promising for a while and consumed attention. It did not matter. Every applied change had a matching revert recorded before onset; the sidecar was detached at 17:47:39Z and the Redis override reverted at 18:04:20Z, the last change in the window and about 57 minutes ahead of onset. Nothing was left in an applied state. The cartservice churn is ambient platform activity, not this incident.

## What traces showed, and the mechanism

Traces were the turning point. The pattern is clean and repeats across the sample: every productcatalogservice GetProduct span carrying ERROR status has exactly one named child call to hipstershop.FeatureFlagService/GetFlag. The handler's own self-time on those spans is negligible, about 0.1-0.2ms, with the flag child consuming nearly the entire parent duration of roughly 0.8-1.7ms. Critically, the flag child spans are themselves not marked ERROR — the error status is first set on the productcatalogservice handler span that wraps the lookup. The handler is reading a flag value and turning it into a returned gRPC error.

The negative controls sit in the same traces, in the same seconds. Sibling GetProduct calls that carry no flag child complete at effectively zero duration with no error. ListProducts, served for recommendationservice, also completes at ~0ms with no error and no flag child. So this is not a service-wide failure and not a total outage of the endpoint; it is a selective, flag-conditioned error path on GetProduct.

The error propagates strictly upward — handler to its gRPC callers (frontend and checkoutservice) and on to the root HTTP span. The degrading hop is consistently the caller-to-handler edge, attributed to error rather than to time spent.

## Alternatives eliminated inside the traces

Several plausible stories were checked against the same trace sample and discarded. Internal handler logic such as catalog parsing or in-process CPU: ruled out, since handler self-time is a fraction of a span dominated by the named child. A hidden uninstrumented database or cache: ruled out, because the flag call is the only child emitted and it accounts for the full parent duration, leaving no unaccounted time. Downstream checkout peers — cartservice/Redis HGET, currencyservice Convert: ruled out, sub-millisecond and error-free in the checkout traces. Caller-specific triggering: ruled out, since failing GetProduct spans appear under both frontend and checkoutservice parents, placing the trigger inside the service's own flag-dependent path.

## Conclusion and remedy

productcatalogservice's GetProduct path fails on a feature-flag condition. The handler performs a flag lookup, the lookup itself succeeds, and the handler converts the returned value into a gRPC error for that request. Requests that skip the lookup succeed normally. Because no release or config change is recorded against the service, the flag value is being held outside the seed service's change scope — which is consistent with both the trace evidence and the empty change log.

The wrong flag value is itself what breaks the request, so the remedy is reverting that flag state rather than rolling back any service artifact. Fix class: config revert. Confidence: medium, for the reasons below.

## Still open

Three gaps hold confidence at medium.

First, a coverage gap. Although the trace query window opened an hour before onset, all ten returned traces start between 19:03:26 and 19:03:37. No trace data exists at or near the stated 19:01:45 onset, so the mechanism is confirmed only for that late window. The onset timestamp itself is unverified by traces.

Second, the unmeasured edge: the feature-flag service was never queried. Who set the flag, when it was set, and its current value are all unknown. Closing that is the single highest-value next step — it both confirms the mechanism and identifies what to revert.

Third, resource exhaustion is unexamined rather than excluded. Logs returned nothing because of the label mismatch, and latency, CPU, memory, and restart series were never returned by any metric query. Nobody has actually looked at those.
