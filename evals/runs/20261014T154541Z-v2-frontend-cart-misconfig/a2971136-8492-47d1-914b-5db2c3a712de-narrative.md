# Checkout order failures traced to shipping's quote hop

## What the responder saw first

The page named checkout, with frontend, frontend-proxy, load-generator and fraud-detection following close behind. Fifteen services were inside the blast radius and the severity was critical. The natural first read — checkout is broken — turned out to be wrong, but it is where the evidence pointed for the first several minutes and it is worth understanding why that read was so convincing: checkout's root span was the first span in every failing trace that carried an error, and everything above it merely repeated that error upward.

The shape of the signal was partial, not total. Checkout's error ratio had been flat at exactly zero for the whole half-hour of baseline, then crossed the alerting threshold roughly two minutes before the nominal incident time and settled around ten percent, with the worst sample at fifty percent. Bursty, not a steady floor. Most orders were still completing.

## Following the error inward (T+0 to T+15m)

Traces were the turn in the investigation. Nine of ten sampled checkout traces in the window failed, and they all failed the same way. The checkout root span for PlaceOrder was marked ERROR with a status message describing a shipping quote failure, and that message in turn cited an HTTP POST to an email service returning 400 where 200 was expected. Inside checkout, exactly one outbound dependency span carried an error: the client span issued under the step that prepares order items and the shipping quote, aimed at shipping's get-quote endpoint.

The interesting part is what the server side of that call looked like. Shipping's get-quote server span was reached, completed in well under a millisecond, and carried no error flag of its own. Whatever went wrong there was rejected instantly. Failing traces ran roughly 13 to 83 milliseconds end to end — shorter than the single healthy trace in the sample at 126ms. Nothing anywhere was slow.

In failing traces checkout reached GetCart and the quote call and then stopped. Currency conversion, product-catalog lookups, payment, the confirmation email, cart emptying, ship-order and the orders publish step simply do not appear. Checkout aborted at the quote step and never got further.

Minutes earlier, in the one healthy trace, the same path resolved cleanly: checkout to shipping, shipping into the quote service, and the email call succeeded. In the failing traces there is no quote-service child span at all.

## Conclusion as it stands

The failure originates in shipping's quote path, not in checkout. The best reading of the evidence is that the destination shipping uses for the quote step is pointed at the wrong service — the email service — which answers immediately with a 400 the quote handler cannot use. The wrongness of that value is the failure. It is not a wait, and it is not an exhaustion: the timings rule both out. The expected fix class is reverting that configuration value.

Confidence is medium, not high, and the reason is stated plainly in the open items below: nobody queried shipping directly. The 400-from-email detail is read only from status text propagated upward.

Checkout, frontend, frontend-proxy and load-generator all carry an inherited error. That is why checkout alerted first despite nothing having been changed against it.

## Dead ends worth keeping

Change history on checkout was queried twice and came back empty both times. That looked like a clean result and partly is — no checkout deploy, config edit, flag flip or quiet rollback happened at or after onset. But the query was scoped to the seed service with zero hops, so no dependency's change history was ever examined, and the window started at the incident timestamp and ran forward about a day. The hours before onset were never looked at. A pre-incident change to shipping is unexamined, not absent. Do not read these empty results as exoneration of anything but checkout-during-and-after.

Checkout's logs returned zero lines of any severity across the full window. Not zero errors — zero lines. A serving checkout emits routine request logging, so this reads as a retrieval problem: wrong label selector, a shipping or ingestion gap, or indexing that excludes the window. Narrowing that query further is wasted effort; the unfiltered full-window form is already the broadest case and it was empty. Whether this is a telemetry pipeline gap or something checkout-side is still unresolved.

Several plausible culprits were examined and discarded on trace evidence. The cart service and its Redis backend complete fast and clean in every failing trace. Payment, currency, product-catalog, flag evaluation and the orders publish step never appear in failing traces, so they cannot have triggered anything. The quote service is not down — failing calls never reach it, and where it does appear, it succeeds. The frontend, proxy and load-generator are carrying inherited flags, not originating failures. And despite the status message naming email, checkout's own email-confirmation call is not implicated: no email span exists in any failing trace. That last one is the easiest trap in this record — the word "email" in the message describes something happening inside shipping, not inside checkout.

## The frontend thread that does not fit

Frontend logs carry two distinct error classes. The second — a gRPC INTERNAL whose detail repeats the shipping-quote-hit-email-and-got-400 string — corroborates the main finding and appears around T+22m by wall clock.

The first does not fit the story. Frontend restarted and came up clean at about 15:45:51, and within a second began emitting gRPC UNAVAILABLE with connection actively refused against 172.18.0.24:7071. That is before the error-ratio change point. The embedded upstream timestamp stays pinned at 15:49:40 across retries running through 15:51:23, which reads as a cached last-known connection error being replayed rather than fresh refusals.

This was examined enough to rule out some readings: it is not DNS or discovery (the address resolved, the host was reachable, nothing was listening on the port), not a timeout (UNAVAILABLE, not DEADLINE_EXCEEDED), not auth or TLS, and not a frontend-local defect — frontend started cleanly and all its error frames describe upstream conditions. What was never established is which service owns 172.18.0.24:7071. This may be a second, separate failure riding alongside the main one. A responder picking this record up should start there.

## Open items

Shipping was never queried directly — no logs, no metrics, no change history. The central claim about a misdirected quote destination rests entirely on status text read out of spans and frontend logs, never confirmed against shipping's own configuration or telemetry. That gap is the single largest reason confidence is medium.

Change history should be re-run with a window that reaches back before onset and with a radius that includes shipping and the email service, since a contract change on the email side would produce the same observable.

The ownership of 172.18.0.24:7071 is unknown and the connection-refused cluster is unexplained.

Checkout's log silence is unresolved and should be chased as a telemetry question independent of this incident.
