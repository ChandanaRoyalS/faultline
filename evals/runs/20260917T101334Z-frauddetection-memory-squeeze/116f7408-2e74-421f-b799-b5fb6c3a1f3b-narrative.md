# frauddetectionservice restart loop after memory ceiling re-applied

## What the page looked like

The alert named exactly one service, frauddetectionservice, at critical severity, and the blast radius never grew beyond it. Nothing else paged, and no unmeasured edges were crossed to reach the suspect — the alerting service and the eventual cause were the same box. Onset is taken as T+0; the record below uses offsets from there.

The first thing to know, months later, is that this incident has no error spike to look at. There is no graph that goes up. The whole case rests on a change record and a log pattern, and the reader should expect to be reasoning from those two things and nothing else.

## First dead end: the error-ratio metric

The instinct was to confirm impact with a service-level error ratio derived from span call counts. That query came back empty — not low, not flat, but with no samples at all. It was then run again over a much wider baseline reaching roughly three hours before onset, and it was equally empty there.

That symmetry is what kills the lead. An empty incident window with a populated baseline would have been a strong signal (telemetry stopping is itself an event). Empty in both directions means the call-count series simply is not being produced under this service name — most likely a pre-existing instrumentation or service-name mismatch. The practical consequence: this metric neither implicates nor clears frauddetectionservice. Do not spend time on it again without first confirming what name the service actually emits span metrics under.

Worth recording explicitly: no container memory working-set, no memory-limit series, no kill-count, no restart-count, no CPU and no heap numbers were ever retrieved. Those queries were never issued. That gap matters for the conclusion below.

## What the logs actually showed

The logs were the turning point. Querying the service's log stream across roughly half an hour either side of onset produced two distinct regimes.

Early in the window (about T-30m) the service looked normal: ordinary order-consumption lines with an incrementing counter, i.e. the consumer loop doing its job.

From roughly T-6m onward, that output stops entirely and is replaced by the same three lines over and over: the JVM picking up its tool options, a benign class-sharing warning, and the OpenTelemetry agent version banner. Nothing else. This triplet recurs about a dozen times through to the end of the window, and after roughly T-4m the interval between successive startups settles to a near-uniform ~62 seconds.

Several candidate readings were tried against this and discarded:
- A failing dependency (broker, downstream RPC, database). Ruled out as unsupportable — there is not one exception, stack trace, host name or client name anywhere in the later lines. There is nothing to cluster on.
- The service being healthy and the problem living in a caller or downstream. Ruled out: the consumption lines are absent from the later window, so the service was not processing at onset.
- A single crash, or a clean rollout restart. Ruled out by the repetition count.
- Graceful shutdown. Ruled out: no termination, drain or consumer-close messages appear at the end of any cycle. Each cycle just stops. That is the signature of an abrupt kill.
- The telemetry agent itself blocking startup. Ruled out: its banner logs successfully at INFO on every single cycle, so it initialises fine each time.
- Load-driven, random crash timing. Ruled out by the ~62s regularity, which reads as a fixed timeout or probe period — a supervisor restarting on a backoff, not entropy.

Net reading: the process dies during or shortly after bootstrap, before the consumer loop produces any output, and is restarted on a fixed cadence.

## The change record

The change log over the preceding 24 hours contained exactly three entries for this service. All three were resource-limit edits to the same container memory limit, all made by the platform-automation actor, and no other resource dimension (CPU, replicas) was touched.

Sequence:
- T-5.0h: memory limit goes from unset to 200m.
- T-4.8h: reverted back to unset, about eleven minutes after it landed.
- T-6m: the same 200m value applied again.

Several framings were checked and dropped. It was not a gradual squeeze — both edits jump straight from unset to the identical 200m, with no intermediate values. It was not flapping — three events in a full day. It was not a code deploy, a release, a feature flag flip or a library bump; no such events exist for the service in the window. It was not a human operator; automation owns all three. And it was not a long-standing setting with something else changing near onset: the limit was unset for the whole ~19 hours before the first edit and unset again from T-4.8h until T-6m. The 200m state existed only in two brief episodes. Nothing at all is recorded at or after onset.

## Conclusion and confidence

About six minutes before onset, automation re-applied a 200m container memory ceiling, taking the limit from unset to 200m. The JVM cannot complete initialisation inside that ceiling: it is killed abruptly during or shortly after bootstrap, the supervisor restarts it, and the cycle repeats on a ~62s backoff without the service ever reaching the order-consumption loop that was producing normal output half an hour earlier. The limit edit is how it started; the kill-restart loop is what is ongoing.

The strongest corroboration is that this exact value was applied once before, five hours earlier, and reverted after eleven minutes. This is a known-bad configuration repeating, not a novel setting.

Confidence is medium, deliberately. Fix class is a configuration revert — put the limit back to unset, as was done at T-4.8h.

## Open questions for whoever reads this next

1. The kill reason is inferred, not observed. No memory working-set, limit, kill-event or restart-count series was ever queried. The chain change-timing plus log-signature is strong but circumstantial; one container-level query would settle it.

2. The error-ratio series is empty in both incident and baseline windows. Whether this service emits span metrics under a different name is unresolved, and until it is, metrics can neither implicate nor clear it.

3. Nobody established why automation re-applied a value that had already been reverted five hours earlier — a scaling recommendation, a reconciliation loop, or configuration drift correction are all plausible and none is in the record. Until that is understood, a revert may simply be re-applied again. This is the highest-value follow-up.

4. Blast radius was scoped to this one service. No one looked at consumers of the fraud-detection order stream, so downstream backlog and caller-visible impact are unmeasured.

5. It was never tested whether 200m sits below the JVM's irreducible heap-plus-metaspace footprint (making any restart futile) or whether the service could survive at 200m under lighter load. That distinction decides whether 200m is wrong in principle or merely wrong today.
