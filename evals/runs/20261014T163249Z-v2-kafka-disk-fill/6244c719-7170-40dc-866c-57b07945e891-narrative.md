# Checkout order placement stalls on broker publish; name resolution the likely cause

## What the page looked like

Three services paged within a minute of each other — checkout, fraud-detection, and accounting — and the visible blast radius eventually covered twelve services. From the responder's chair the first impression was misleading: the alert fan-out suggested a broad platform problem, and nine of the edges crossed during triage had no measurement on them at all, so there was no cheap way to tell propagation from origin. The user-visible symptom was a timeout at the edge, not an error page from a backend. Treat the twelve-service radius as an artifact of shared dependency on order placement, not as twelve independent problems.

## First moves, and the first dead end

The instinct was to read checkout's own account of itself. That failed completely. An unfiltered log query against the checkout service label over the ~32 minutes around onset returned not a single line — not an error, not a warning, not routine informational chatter. Severity filtering was not the explanation (the query had no level filter), and window width was not the explanation (32 minutes is wide enough that a healthy service would emit something). Roughly T+4m was spent re-running and widening this before accepting that the log path was simply not going to answer anything here. Whether checkout was genuinely silent or the collection/labelling path was broken was never settled, and it remains the single largest hole in this record.

## The metrics detour

Next came checkout's span-metrics error ratio, on the assumption that a surge would point at a failing dependency. It pointed the other way. During the investigation window the error ratio averaged under one percent with a peak around six percent, and the comparison flagged no sustained departure. More confusingly, the preceding baseline window was *worse* — roughly twelve percent mean with excursions to fifty percent. Read naively that says the window under investigation is the recovery, not the onset. This cost time around T+9m. The lesson for the next responder: this query aggregated by service name only, so it carried no per-dependency breakdown, no request rate, and no latency percentiles. A dependency that is slow but ultimately successful is invisible to it — which is exactly the shape of failure that was actually occurring. The low error ratio did usefully close one door: hard failures propagating up from downstream were not happening.

## Traces, where the answer was

The trace sample broke the case at roughly T+15m. Four failing checkout traces (of ten matching; the result was truncated at 200 spans) all showed the same shape: the Kafka order-publish span, a direct child of checkout's PlaceOrder handler, holding essentially the entire trace duration — 52 to 58 seconds of self time. Every sibling span finished in single-digit milliseconds: cart, product-catalog, payment Charge, currency, quote, shipping, flagd, Redis commands, SQL queries. The publish span began only 20–25ms after PlaceOrder started, so the stall was present from the first moment of order placement rather than emerging after other work.

Two failure modes appeared across the sample. Two traces showed the broker hostname failing to resolve through the container's DNS resolver. The other two showed the client-side circuit breaker already open. The ordering matters: the breaker is a consequence of repeated failed broker connections, not the origin. Resetting it would have achieved nothing.

The edge behaviour also explains the alert pattern. The load-generator and frontend-proxy ingress spans errored at about 15.0 seconds — a proxy timeout — while checkout kept waiting on the publish for another 37 to 43 seconds after the proxy had given up. That is why the symptom looked like a timeout and why checkout's own error ratio stayed low.

## Looking for a change, and not finding one

With a connectivity story forming, the obvious next question was what changed. Nothing did, as far as the change log knows. Queries against checkout, against the broker, and against flagd all came back entirely empty across a 24-hour span — no deployments, no configuration edits, no environment-variable changes, no flag flips, no restarts or renames. That rules out rollback as a remediation path for checkout: there is no recent change to roll back.

One caveat that a later reader should not miss: the checkout change-log window was constructed forward from the onset timestamp rather than backward. It therefore covers the day *after* onset well and the pre-onset period poorly. A pre-incident edit to the broker address or to service discovery is only weakly excluded by this evidence, not firmly excluded.

## Dead ends worth keeping

Four lines of inquiry consumed time and produced nothing usable.

Broker health via span metrics: the error-ratio query for the broker returned zero samples in the incident window *and* zero in the baseline window. The temptation is to read the emptiness as the broker dying at onset; the baseline emptiness kills that reading. This is an instrumentation coverage gap that predates the incident, and the series cannot distinguish a healthy broker from a dead one. Do not re-run it.

Fraud-detection logs: a substring search for broker-related lines in fraud-detection over the same window returned nothing. No connection errors, no consumer rebalances, no producer retries, no offset-commit failures. It could not be used to anchor the timeline either.

Downstream RPCs and data stores: ruled out directly by the trace spans — payment, cart, catalog, currency, quote, shipping, flagd all healthy, SQL and Redis under a millisecond.

Frontend and proxy as origin: their spans have near-zero self time and error only at the 15-second boundary, well after the publish span had already stalled.

## Conclusion and confidence

Checkout is running and instrumented but cut off from its broker endpoint, hanging well past the ~15 second proxy timeout that produces the user-visible failure. The originating detail visible in traces is that the broker hostname does not resolve through the container DNS resolver; the open circuit breaker is downstream of that. With no recorded change to any relevant artifact or value, this reads as a loss of connectivity or name resolution rather than something that was altered. Fix class: reconnect. Confidence is medium, and the reasons for that are in the next section.

## Still open — start here next time

Scope was never tested. No resolution or broker-reachability probe was run from any service other than checkout, so whether this is environment-wide or confined to checkout's pod and its resolver is unknown. That single probe would most cheaply raise confidence from medium.

Broker health is unestablished. Its span metrics are empty on both sides of onset and no broker logs were queried at all.

Checkout's silence in logs is unresolved — pipeline gap or genuine silence. Either answer is operationally important.

The pre-onset change picture for checkout is weakly covered because of the forward-facing query window. Re-run it backward from onset before concluding that nothing was edited.
