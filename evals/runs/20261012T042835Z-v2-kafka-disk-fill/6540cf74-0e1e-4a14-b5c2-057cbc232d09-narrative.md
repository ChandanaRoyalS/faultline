# Checkout stalls on its order-publish hop to the message broker

## What paged, and what the first look showed

The page was on checkout, and checkout alone. Nine services sat inside the blast radius and eight edges between them had no measurement at all, so the first job was to find out whether checkout was the sick service or the loudest one.

The shape visible at T+0 was unhelpful in a specific way: nobody was returning errors. Checkout's error ratio over the window sat at roughly 1% mean against a ~0.9% baseline, a 1.1x shift with comparable spread, and the one change point flagged near the end of the window crossed a threshold the baseline window had already reached on its own. Frontend's error ratio was flat zero for all 129 samples, strictly below its own baseline of ~0.5%. Neither service was erroring more than usual. Whatever was wrong was presenting as requests that never came back, not requests that came back wrong.

Note for anyone reading the metric evidence later: no request-rate or p95 latency series was returned for any service in this incident. The onset timing in this record rests entirely on the trace sample and on that one marginal change point. Treat the timeline as approximate.

## Where the time actually went

The traces settled it. Ten traces, 200 spans, with a checkout PlaceOrder span present as a child of the frontend gRPC call in every sampled trace — so checkout was up, serving, and exporting the whole time.

The durations were the tell. The load-generator root span and the frontend-proxy ingress span both ended at almost exactly 15.0s with an error status, while the checkout PlaceOrder span underneath them ran on for 77s, 93s, 115s and 126s in the four sampled traces. The client gave up at its deadline; the server kept waiting for sixty to a hundred and ten seconds past that point. This is why frontend's error ratio stayed at zero while the user-facing experience was broken — the proxy was absorbing the timeouts.

Inside checkout, in three of four traces, effectively the entire duration sat in one child span, checkout/orders publish, self-time dominant at 76.9s, 93.2s and 114.8s, carrying ERROR. In the fourth there was no publish child emitted at all and the ~126s was self-time on PlaceOrder itself — the same stall, one span shallower.

The error strings on those publish spans are the most concrete evidence in the whole record: one trace carries a message-broker host that failed DNS resolution; two carry circuit-breaker-open rejections. Read in order, that is a broker whose name stopped resolving, followed by the producer's breaker tripping on the accumulated failures.

## Dead ends worth keeping

**The six synchronous dependencies.** The obvious theory — something downstream of checkout went slow and checkout is just the first place it shows — does not survive the traces. cart GetCart/EmptyCart 0.3–3.3ms, currency Convert 0.6–5.3ms, email send_order_confirmation 3–6ms, payment Charge 0.7–1.5ms, product-catalog GetProduct 0.6–2.5ms, shipping get-quote/ship-order 0.1–5.2ms. No errors on any of them in any sampled trace. The whole synchronous preparation phase finished in 13–59ms, under 0.1% of request time. The product-catalog SQL spans were 0.2–2.5ms, so the catalog database is not it either. Payment authorisation was a live theory for a few minutes and died the same way: sub-millisecond, clean status.

**Broad resource starvation inside checkout.** Tempting, because checkout is the one service behaving badly. Ruled out by the shape: time is concentrated as self-time in a single span at 100% of trace duration while every sibling stays at normal millisecond latency. Starvation would smear.

**The frontend or the proxy as the originator.** Also tempting, because the proxy is where the errors appear. But the proxy and the client root both terminate at 15.00s while the backend span runs on for another minute or two. The proxy is timing out on something that never returns; it is not the source.

**Any change to checkout.** Two independent reads of change history over a 24-hour window came back completely empty for checkout — no deploy, no image swap, no env or config edit, no flag-store mutation, no rollback. There is no release to correlate onset against, and rolling checkout back is not an available lever because there is no prior version recorded. Both reads were scoped to the seed service only, zero hops, which matters below.

**Checkout's own logs.** A query for every line from checkout across the window returned nothing at all — not zero errors, zero lines, including routine request and startup chatter. That closed several avenues at once: no connection-error line naming a failing dependency, no parse or write-path message, no upstream status codes to attribute blame with. An empty log here is not a clean bill of health; a service that normally emits baseline traffic and emits nothing is itself anomalous. This dead end never resolved — see below.

## The reading that fits

Checkout hangs on its order-publish hop to the message broker. The producer waits out its delivery timeout while callers abandon at 15s; the broker's hostname no longer resolves and packets to it are being dropped rather than refused, which is why the producer waits instead of failing fast. The breaker then opens and later attempts are rejected outright, which is what the two circuit-breaker spans show.

Confidence is medium, not high. The strongest supporting detail — the broker's own log, which under this reading should be writing a repeating telemetry-export failure line for the whole duration — was never fetched. Nothing in this investigation read the broker's logs or state at all. The remediation class that fits is reconnecting the broker to the network its producers share; that action is catalogued but the executor cannot perform it, so any proposal naming it must say so rather than expect it to run. Restart, rollback and config-revert are the only executable actions, and none of them addresses this mechanism: there is no change to revert and no prior image to roll back to.

## Still open when this was written

Four things a later responder should pick up rather than assume.

First, the broker was never inspected directly. The conclusion about how it was cut off rests on two span error strings and on what the class is known to look like elsewhere — not on the broker's own logs, which were not fetched.

Second, checkout returned zero log lines for the entire window and nobody explained why. Either collection is broken for that stream, the lines live under a label nobody queried, or the service genuinely wrote nothing while blocked in the publish path. All three remain live.

Third, change coverage is thin in a way that matters. Both change queries were scoped to checkout only. There is no change coverage for the broker or for any of checkout's dependencies, so a broker-side config or deploy change is not excluded — it is simply unexamined.

Fourth, the absent rate and latency series. Without them the onset time is a guess built from four sampled traces and one marginal change point. If this recurs, pull p95 and request rate for checkout and the broker's consumers first; it would turn the timeline from inference into measurement.
