# Unresolved critical incident: error burst on productcatalogservice, origin unestablished

## What the board looked like when I sat down

Four alerts landed together and formed the cluster I am calling T+0: frontend, loadgenerator, productcatalogservice, and checkoutservice (the last a few seconds later, at T+15s). Severity critical, blast radius 12 services, and the triage entry point handed to me was frontend. Four edges in the affected span were unmeasured and stayed that way for the whole investigation.

The honest summary up front: I did not establish a cause. I can name one service on timing alone and nothing more. If you are reading this months later hoping for a mechanism, there isn't one in here — but the dead ends below will save you an hour.

## Frontend was the entry point and turned out to be a red herring

Because triage started at frontend, my first pull was its aggregate error ratio against the immediately preceding baseline. It moved the wrong way for a suspect: the mean fell to roughly a quarter of baseline, and no sustained departure was detected. There are short peaks inside the incident window, but they are smaller than peaks already present in the baseline period, so they are ordinary for this service.

Three things that this rules out, and which you should not re-litigate: frontend is not the visible symptom surface (its error volume went down); there is no frontend error onset to align with anything downstream, so a directly-propagating, user-visible failure chain is not what happened; and a frontend-side regression at window start is inconsistent with error behaviour that improved across the boundary.

What this pull could not do — and this matters — is name a downstream. It is service-aggregate only, with no per-dependency breakdown. So the question "which call out of frontend is sick" was never actually answered, only made harder to ask.

## The one positive signal, and why it is weaker than it first appears

productcatalogservice shows a discrete error-ratio change point at T-2m, about two minutes ahead of the alert cluster. That timing is the entire basis for naming this service.

Measured against a 32-minute baseline the spike looks dramatic: the baseline is flat at exactly zero across all 128 samples, standard deviation zero, and then the incident window carries a mean near 1.4% with a peak around 12.5%. Read alone, that says "new failure mode."

I then widened the baseline to three hours, and the picture deflated. Over the longer comparison the service shows bursts of comparable double-digit magnitude *before* the incident, and its mean during the incident window is actually lower than the mean of the equal-length baseline — roughly 0.58x. The change point at T-2m is still real and still crosses the threshold, but the burst is not unprecedented for this service. My earlier read of "novel" was an artefact of picking a short, unusually quiet baseline.

Both views agree on the shape: intermittent and spiky, not continuous. The floor stays at zero, standard deviation exceeds the mean, and the peak is nowhere near total failure — the large majority of calls succeeded throughout. So: not hard-down, not a crash loop with no traffic, not a smooth worsening ramp, and not a chronic pre-existing condition in the narrow view. Timing-consistent with onset; not clearly abnormal in magnitude.

## Dead end: the change log, queried in the wrong direction

I pulled the change history for productcatalogservice twice, and both times it came back completely empty — no deployments, no config edits, no flag flips. For a while I treated that as a real negative and wrote off same-service change as a trigger.

It is not a real negative. Both queries ran from the alert timestamp forward for about twenty-four hours. They cover the period *after* onset, not the lead-up. The emptiness legitimately excludes a post-onset mitigating or reverting change to this service (nothing was logged in that span, so recovery, if any, was not change-driven), but it says nothing whatsoever about whether something landed in the hours before T+0.

If you pick this up: re-run the change history over the twenty-four hours *preceding* T+0, for productcatalogservice and for its dependencies. That is the single cheapest open item on the board.

## Dead end: logs that were never really queried

The log pull for productcatalogservice returned zero lines across the full window. That looked like meaningful silence — possibly a service too wedged to log.

It was a selector problem. The label value used was hyphenated ("product-catalog-service") while the service name in scope is unhyphenated ("productcatalogservice"). Zero rows is at least as consistent with a mismatched selector as with genuine absence. Narrowing the time range around the T-2m burst would not have helped; the window already spanned the whole period and the issue sits upstream of time entirely.

So there is no stack trace, no named downstream, no file or query pinpointing anything — and equally, no basis to claim the service was silent. This result is uninformative rather than negative. Verify the label value against what the log store actually carries, then re-run.

## What was never looked at

No latency percentiles, no CPU, no memory series were retrieved for any service in this investigation. Every metric pull evaluated the error-ratio template only. That means the T-2m burst cannot be correlated with saturation or slowness in either direction — not confirmed, not excluded.

No per-dependency breakdown exists for any service, so the actual edge that carried the problem is unidentified.

checkoutservice alerted at T+15s and was never examined; there is no dispatch for it on this board at all. The four unmeasured edges were likewise never crossed with instrumentation. loadgenerator alerted and was not investigated.

## Where I left it

Conclusion: productcatalogservice is named on timing alone — a change point two minutes ahead of the alert cluster — with no evidence for what is wrong inside it. Confidence low. No fix class identified. Frontend is ruled out as origin.

Open, in the order I would work them:

1. Change history for the twenty-four hours *before* T+0, productcatalogservice and its dependencies. Both existing queries covered the wrong side of onset.
2. Logs for productcatalogservice using a verified label value. The existing empty result is a selector artefact and proves nothing.
3. Latency, CPU and memory around T-2m, and a first look at checkoutservice to explain why it alerted at T+15s. Nothing on this board speaks to any of it.
