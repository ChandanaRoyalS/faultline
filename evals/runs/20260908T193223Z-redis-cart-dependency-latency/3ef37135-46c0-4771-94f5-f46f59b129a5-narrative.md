# Cart latency traced to fixed per-cache-operation delay

## What the responder saw first

The page arrived as a warning-severity latency alert with cartservice at the centre and frontend, loadgenerator and checkoutservice alerting alongside it. Twelve services were inside the blast radius, and four of the edges crossed while walking outward had no measurement attached at all, so the early picture was assembled mostly from the shape of the alert fan rather than from numbers.

The fan itself was the first useful hint. frontend, checkoutservice and loadgenerator all sit upstream of cart operations, and none of them alerted on anything but time. Nothing was erroring. That framed the question from T+0 as "where is the waiting happening" rather than "what broke".

## The metrics detour (a dead end worth keeping)

The first instinct was to anchor onset with a metric series and compare the incident window against a clean baseline. This consumed real time and produced nothing.

The error-ratio query for cartservice, keyed on the service_name label, returned no samples — not in the incident window, and not in the four-hour baseline before it either. The comparison tooling dutifully reported "no sustained departure from baseline", which is a vacuous answer: an empty series is not a flat healthy series, and it cannot be used to clear the service. A narrower half-hour window was tried and came back equally empty.

Two tempting misreadings were discarded here. First, that telemetry stopping marked the onset — it did not, because the pre-incident baseline is just as empty, so there was never a populated series to lose. Second, that the selector was correct and the service was simply quiet — the selector matched nothing across a full hour spanning both windows, which means the label set queried is not the one that actually exists for this service. Anyone repeating this investigation should not carry service_name into the duration, request-rate, restart-count or replica-readiness queries. Those four questions were never answered.

## Logs: degradation, not unavailability

Logs were the first evidence that actually moved things forward. Cart-service was emitting ordinary application request handling — add, get, empty cart — at both ends of the window, with no error lines, no exceptions, no startup banner and no reconnect chatter. It was still serving continuously through the last retained line.

That killed four hypotheses in one pass: crash loop or restart (an unbroken stream of request logs with no initialization output), failure to start, a hard cache connection or authentication break (operations were completing successfully, only slowly), and total outage.

The one positive signal was timing. In the early retained segment, the follow-up cart read logged single-digit milliseconds after the preceding write. In the late segment, the same pairing consistently logged about 600ms apart. Same operations, same order, same success — just stretched.

The important limitation: the log result was truncated to the oldest handful and newest few dozen lines of a roughly four-hour window. Roughly 15:46 through 19:37 is simply unobserved. Onset timing could not be established from logs, and still cannot.

## Traces: where the time actually went

Traces settled it. Across ten traces and thirty-seven spans, essentially all cartservice latency sits in leaf cache-operation spans — HGET and HMSET — each around 301 to 307ms, almost entirely self-time. The gRPC handler spans, GetCart and AddItem, contribute under a millisecond of self-time each.

The arithmetic decomposes cleanly. The ~600ms seen on writes is not one slow operation; it is two sequential cache operations inside a single AddItem, a read followed by a write, the second starting about 303 to 305ms after the first, with no span exceeding ~308ms. Read-only GetCart paths total ~302 to 308ms and contain exactly one cache round trip. The ~912 to 916ms end-user POSTs are an AddItem (two operations, ~605ms) followed by a frontend GetCart (~305ms), with frontend self-time of only 1 to 4ms per span.

So the penalty is per cache round trip, fixed, and not a per-request or per-handler constant. The spread across all ten traces is roughly 300.8 to 307.4ms — tight enough to look like a fixed added delay rather than load-dependent queueing.

Ruled out along the way: latency inside cartservice's own handler (sub-millisecond self-time); a downstream service dragging it down (the only children of cartservice handler spans anywhere are cache operations, no other service appears beneath it); the frontend or its gRPC client layer stalling (child spans start within ~2ms of the parent); writes-only degradation (reads are equally slow); and the idea that only the full frontend-originated path is affected — standalone cartservice-rooted traces, including bare cache-read roots with no frontend involvement, show the same ~300ms.

## The change record, and why it is uncomfortable

The change log for cartservice covering the day before onset contains fourteen entries, every one attributed to platform-automation, and every one forming an apply-then-revert pair: three image-reference updates to a hotfix tag (each reverted, the last about six hours before onset), two REDIS_ADDR environment edits pointing the cache at an alternate port (each reverted, at ~13.6h and ~49m before onset), and two traffic-shaping sidecar attachments adding fixed egress delay on the cart-service network namespace (each removed, at ~14.0h and ~1.1h before onset).

Read literally, the record clears itself. No altered image was live at onset. Both cache-endpoint overrides were reverted. Both delay-adding sidecars were removed. The nearest entry to onset is a revert, not an apply, so there is no change close enough in time to be a proximate trigger, and no human actor appears anywhere in the record.

But the record is marked untrusted, and the repeating apply/revert cadence with identical before/after values reads as scheduled platform automation exercising this exact path. The traces show a fixed ~300ms penalty on precisely the cache network path that those sidecars targeted. The most economical explanation is that a delay rule on the cart-service network namespace was still in effect at onset despite the log claiming otherwise — but that is an inference against an untrusted source, not a confirmation.

## Conclusion and confidence

cartservice latency is entirely consumed by its cache round trips. Each round trip carries a near-constant ~300ms penalty, identical on reads and writes; handler work is negligible; operations continue to succeed. frontend, checkoutservice and loadgenerator alerted only because they were blocked waiting on cartservice. This is a wait, not a failure.

Fix class is a configuration revert on the cart cache network path. Confidence is medium, and the medium rating comes entirely from the change record being untrusted and self-contradicting the trace evidence.

## Still open for the next responder

Three things a future investigation should close.

First, verify directly against the live cart-service network namespace whether a delay-adding sidecar or traffic-shaping rule was actually active at onset. The change log claims all were removed and is untrusted; the traces say something fixed was still adding ~300ms per round trip. Inspect the namespace rather than the log.

Second, onset timing is unknown. Logs are truncated across roughly 15:46 to 19:37 and no cartservice latency series was retrievable. Before retrying metrics, discover the label set that actually exists for this service — service_name matched nothing across a full hour.

Third, the health of the cache backend itself was never measured. No CPU, memory, saturation or restart data was collected for redis-cart, so intrinsically slow cache behaviour is not excluded as an alternative to an externally applied delay. The uniformity of the penalty argues against it, but nothing observed rules it out.
