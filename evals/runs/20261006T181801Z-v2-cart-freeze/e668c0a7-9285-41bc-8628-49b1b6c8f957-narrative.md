# Intermittent 15-second stalls inside the frontend handler

## What was visible first

The page opened with frontend-proxy and the load-generator alerting together, three services in the blast radius, severity critical. The proxy error ratio was the cleanest early signal: a baseline of exactly zero across every sample, rising to a mean near 4.4% with a peak around 15%. Two features of that series shaped everything after. It was bursty — standard deviation exceeded the mean and the minimum stayed at zero, so clean traffic interleaved with spikes. And there were two separate threshold crossings, which rules out a single step change that stuck. At worst roughly 85% of calls still succeeded, so nobody should have been chasing a hard outage.

The frontend's own error ratio looked almost innocent: a tiny mean, brief spikes near 4%, most samples at zero, and no sustained departure from baseline. That reading cost time. The only tell was that the pre-incident baseline was exactly zero, so any error at all was new in kind. Do not conclude from a quiet frontend error counter that the frontend is healthy — it was not recording the timeout itself.

## The traces, where the picture turns

Ten traces came back, 116 spans. Exactly two failed — an add-to-cart POST and a view-cart GET, about four minutes before the alert and thirty seconds apart — both lasting almost exactly 15.00s, the signature of a fixed client deadline rather than variable latency.

The span structure is the finding. The chain runs load-generator, proxy ingress, proxy router egress, frontend handler — and stops. The frontend span is a leaf with no children at all and roughly 15002ms of self-time. The proxy spans carry sub-millisecond self-time yet hold the error status; the frontend span does not. The error is recorded where the deadline was enforced, not where the time was spent. With no child spans, the frontend never dispatched a downstream call: it stalled before or during dispatch.

Two details kill the alternatives. A sibling product-catalog branch inside the same failing trace completed in 4.3ms, so the frontend was serving other work at that instant. And all eight non-failing traces finished in 6–52ms, interleaved in time with the stalls. No ramp, nothing in between — either fast success or a full 15 seconds. One POST and one GET fail identically, so it is not method-specific either.

## Dead ends worth keeping

The shipping-quote/email signature is the most seductive wrong turn. Frontend logs carried a dense, identical repeating gRPC status 13 INTERNAL naming a shipping quote that failed on a POST to email returning 400 — the whole burst inside about fifteen milliseconds, roughly fourteen minutes before the alert. It looks like a complete story. A 400 means the peer answered, so it cannot produce a 15-second hang, and no shipping, quote, checkout or email span appears anywhere in the 116 spans examined. Going to the email service directly confirmed it: every line a 200, latencies in the low milliseconds, no degradation. Separate, lower-severity issue; triage it apart.

Change history was the second dead end, and partly a self-inflicted one. Frontend and email both returned explicit empty sets — genuine 'no changes', not 'no data' — so there is nothing to roll back. Shipping had eight entries, all automation-authored image-reference toggles that begin and end on a revert, net state unchanged, the last of them 3.3 hours before onset. Critically, all three change windows started at the alert timestamp and ran forward, so the pre-onset hours were never examined.

What remains open: no frontend resource metrics were ever collected — heap, CPU, event-loop lag, pool utilisation, fd counts are all unmeasured — so the working explanation (handlers intermittently blocking on an exhausted local resource until the 15s deadline fires) is inferred, not observed. Confidence is low. Whether the frontend logged anything at all during the two stalls is also unknown, leaving a silent-process explanation unexcluded. Suggested first action is a restart, with resource instrumentation added before the next occurrence.
