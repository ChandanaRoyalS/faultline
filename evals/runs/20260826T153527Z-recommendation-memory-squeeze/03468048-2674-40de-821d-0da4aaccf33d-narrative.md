# Frontend and Load Generator Paging Together — Mechanism Not Established

## What was visible, in order

The first thing on the board was the load generator alerting. Fifteen seconds later the frontend alerted. That ordering matters and is the single most suggestive raw observation in the whole record: the synthetic traffic source noticed something before the user-facing service did, which is the shape you see when the frontend is restating a failure that originates behind it rather than producing one itself. Triage put the blast radius at seven services with the frontend as the nominal start point, severity critical, and flagged one edge inside that radius as unmeasured — meaning there is a hop in the call graph we have no telemetry across at all.

From there the investigation went narrow. Four dispatches were sent, all of them asking the same question of the change history: did anything land on frontend, adservice, cartservice, or checkoutservice? All four came back empty. No metric, log, trace, or resource dispatch was ever run. So the record that follows is almost entirely a record of absences, and the reader should hold that firmly: we know a little about what did not happen and nothing about what did.

## The four change-history dispatches and what they actually cover

Each of the four services was queried against the change log and each returned nothing — no deploys, no config or environment edits, no feature-flag flips. On its face that eliminates the most common opening hypothesis for a critical incident: somebody shipped something.

But every one of the four specialists independently flagged the same limitation, and it is the detail most likely to be forgotten later. The window actually searched was roughly fifteen minutes wide — about ten minutes before onset and five after — not the full hour that was asked for. So the interval from roughly fifty minutes before onset up to ten minutes before onset was never looked at, for any of the four services. A change that landed in that gap and took time to manifest would be invisible to everything recorded here. The empty result is real; its reach is much shorter than it looks.

Three of the seven services in the blast radius were never queried at all. Nor was anything at the cluster or orchestration layer, where limits, quotas and container-level ceilings are edited — those edits routinely do not appear in per-service change logs.

## Dead ends worth keeping

These are the branches that were closed, and they are worth reading before repeating them.

A frontend deploy or rollout at onset: excluded within the searched window. A frontend flag flip: excluded, same log covers flips. A frontend config or environment change: excluded. Consequently, rolling back a recent frontend release was ruled out as an obvious first move — there is no artifact in the immediate window to revert to.

Same result for adservice: no deploy, no flip, no config push in the searched window, and therefore no target for a service-level rollback owned by that team. Same for cartservice, with the added closure that an in-progress rollback or remediation was not itself the disturbance, since any such action would have shown up as a recorded change. Same for checkoutservice, and because that window extends five minutes past onset while remaining empty, a partially rolled-out change still progressing during the incident is also excluded.

What all of this adds up to: a narrow band of triggers is ruled out for four of seven services over fifteen minutes. That is genuinely useful and genuinely small.

## The tempting prior, and why it was not adopted

A prior incident surfaced during retrieval and it rhymes uncomfortably well with this one: load generator and frontend paging together, adservice implicated only by the absence of anything wrong in its change history, and the actual mechanism turning out to be a container memory ceiling lowered below the JVM's real footprint, producing repeated kills. The alert ordering here matches that pattern almost beat for beat.

It was deliberately not adopted as the conclusion. Nothing gathered in this investigation tests it. There is no memory-against-limit reading, no restart count, no kill event, no saturation figure for any service in the radius. Treating the prior as the answer would be substituting a previous responder's measurement for a present one, and the resemblance is precisely the kind of thing that makes that substitution feel safe. Note the practical consequence: if the mechanism does turn out to be a lowered ceiling, the correct remediation is reverting that limit, not rolling back any service artifact — and those two actions point at different teams and different systems. That choice cannot be made until something is measured.

## Conclusion and confidence

Not established. Confidence low. No fix class assigned, because no mechanism was identified. The only positive findings in this record are absences, and each absence is itself qualified by a window shorter than requested and a service list shorter than the blast radius.

## Still open — pick up here

What is the failure signature? No error-rate, latency, or restart data exists for any of the seven services. This is the first thing to collect and it should have been the first thing collected.

Is anything in the radius being killed for memory or restarting? Memory-against-limit plus container restart counts would confirm or kill the resource-ceiling hypothesis outright in one dispatch.

Did a change land for any of the four queried services in the unexamined stretch, roughly fifty to ten minutes before onset? Re-run the change queries over the full hour.

Were there changes to the three blast-radius services never queried, or at the cluster and orchestration layer? Limit and quota edits frequently live only there.

What is the unmeasured edge triage flagged, and does the failure propagate across it? Until that hop has telemetry, the topology story is incomplete by construction.

Is the frontend the origin or just the first place users could see it? The load generator alerting fifteen seconds earlier argues for the latter, and if so the true start point is somewhere downstream and has not been named.
