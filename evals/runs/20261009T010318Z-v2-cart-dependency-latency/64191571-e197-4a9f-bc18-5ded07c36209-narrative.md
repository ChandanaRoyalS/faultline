# Slow checkout path traced to a traffic-shaping attachment on cart's network namespace

## What the responder sees first

The page arrives as a warning-severity event with a broad but shallow blast radius: fourteen services touched, the walk starting at cart, and five services alerting — cart, frontend, frontend-proxy, checkout, and load-generator. Taken at face value this reads like a cart outage pulling its callers down with it. That framing is wrong in an important way, and the first twenty minutes of this investigation were spent discovering how.

The alerting set is the clue that matters. Four of the five alerting services are cart's callers or the edge in front of them. Cart itself alerts, but as will become clear, nothing cart can observe about itself is abnormal. Hold that shape in mind: the symptom lives with the callers, the cause lives at cart's boundary.

## Cart looks healthy from the inside

Three independent looks at cart all came back clean, and each one closed off a plausible story.

Logs across the hour before onset are uniformly info-level entries from the Valkey-backed cart store — GetCart, AddItem, EmptyCart — with sub-second spacing at both ends of the window. No exceptions, no connection refusals, no pool exhaustion, no startup or shutdown banners. Cart did not crash, did not restart, and did not lose its store. One caveat worth recording: the log result was truncated to the oldest eight and newest thirty-two lines, so the moment of onset itself falls in the omitted middle. The boundaries are clean; the exact instant is inferred.

Traces told the same story more precisely. Cart's server spans run from a tenth of a millisecond to about nine milliseconds, with handler self-time under one millisecond. Its egress is equally quick: Redis HGET/HMSET/EXPIRE calls are nearly all sub-millisecond, and the flagd boolean resolution sits between one and four milliseconds across four observations. Nothing in cart's own work approaches three hundred milliseconds.

Error metrics were the third look, and the most counterintuitive. Cart's error ratio is flat at exactly zero through the window — not elevated, but roughly a ninth of its ~1% baseline mean. Peak error ratio did not grow either. Cart was emitting proportionally fewer errors during the incident than before it.

## Dead ends worth keeping

These are the branches that were walked and closed. A future responder on a similar page can skip them, or — more usefully — can recognise when the same clean readings mean the same thing.

*Cart as the error source.* Ruled out twice over. Zero error-status spans means there is no refusal mode here, and no upstream failure is surfacing into cart as failed spans either. Also ruled out: the theory that added latency crossed a timeout threshold and converted into errors. It did not; the error line never moved upward.

*Redis saturation.* If the cart store were saturated, every cache operation would carry uniform added cost. Instead Redis spans are predominantly sub-millisecond with exactly one ~9ms outlier — which, note, appeared inside a checkout trace and briefly looked interesting before being set aside as two orders of magnitude short of what we were hunting.

*The flagd lookup stalling.* Four flagd-rooted cart traces complete end to end in one and a half to five milliseconds, service side under a millisecond. Closed.

*A frontend change.* The change log for frontend is empty across a full day-long window — no deploy, no rollback, no edit to how frontend addresses cart. There is nothing on the frontend side to revert, which is what pushed the investigation back toward cart's dependency boundary.

*Cart as the degrading hop in the slow traces.* This one is the most seductive dead end. The slowest sampled trace, a checkout at around 182ms at the root, attributes its degrading hop to load-generator self-time, not cart. Across the remaining samples the flagged degrading hops are load-generator or frontend/product-catalog — never cart. Reading only the trace sample, you would walk away from cart entirely.

*A per-call 300ms penalty visible in cart's spans.* Explicitly not present in the sampled traffic. There is no cluster near 300ms anywhere in cart's egress.

## The change record, and the turn

The change history for cart is the only place anything is moving. It contains no deployment, no release, no runtime config edit, and no flag flip — only five platform-automation events that attach and remove a traffic-shaping container from cart's network namespace. When attached, that container applies a fixed 300ms egress delay on eth0 with jitter explicitly at zero.

The events form a repeating cycle — attach, remove, attach, remove, attach — across roughly the forty-five minutes before onset. The final event is an attach at T-4m with no matching removal. The shaping was therefore in force at onset and had not been cleaned up.

Every one of the five events is attributed to the same automated actor, so there is no human operator to interview. And because the delay is constant with zero jitter, any variability in observed latency has to come from somewhere other than this.

This reconciles the contradiction. A delay applied at the network namespace boundary on egress is paid on the wire, after cart's handler has finished and after its own span has closed. Cart's self-time, its Redis path, and its flagd path are all genuinely fast — the penalty lands between cart and whoever called it. That is why cart is simultaneously blameless in every one of its own signals and the origin of the blast radius. The callers — frontend, frontend-proxy, checkout, load-generator — are the ones holding a slow socket, and they are exactly the services that alerted.

## Conclusion and remediation class

A platform-automation traffic-shaping container attached to cart's network namespace at T-4m, applying a constant 300ms egress delay on eth0, and was still attached at onset. Every cart response pays that penalty in the wire path, invisible to cart's own instrumentation, and surfaces as latency at cart's callers along the checkout path.

Confidence is medium, not high — see the open items below. Fix class is a configuration revert: detach the shaping container and confirm the attach/remove cycle does not re-arm.

## Open questions and gaps in the evidence

These are the honest holes. The conclusion rests on a change record plus the absence of any competing explanation, not on direct observation of the step.

First and most important: no latency metric for cart was ever retrieved. Both metric dispatches ran the error-ratio template only. The ~300ms p50/p95 step is inferred from the change record; it was never measured.

Second: the trace sample is narrow. All ten returned traces start within a roughly three-and-a-half-minute band well before onset, leaving both the earlier and later portions of the requested window unobserved — including everything from the attach at T-4m onward. A trace query bounded tightly to the attach-through-onset interval would confirm or refute a step at the caller-to-cart hop. That query was not run.

Third: nothing was dispatched against frontend, frontend-proxy, checkout, or load-generator telemetry. The claim that their symptoms are purely downstream of cart rests on topology reasoning alone.

Fourth: why cart's error ratio fell to zero rather than holding at its ~1% baseline is unexplained. A reduction in completed call volume under added latency is one reading, but request rate was never measured, so this remains speculation.

Fifth: it is not established whether the shaping applies to egress only or in both directions, nor whether it covers all cart replicas or just one. This directly bounds how much of the user-visible latency the cause actually accounts for.

Sixth: triage reported fourteen unmeasured edges. No evidence excludes an independent contributor on the product-catalog or frontend hops — which is notable precisely because the sampled traces flagged those hops as degrading.
