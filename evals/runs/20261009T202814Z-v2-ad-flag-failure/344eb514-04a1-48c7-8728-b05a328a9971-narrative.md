# Partial gRPC UNAVAILABLE responses from the ad service

## What was visible, in order

The page came from ad: a minority of GetAds calls returning gRPC UNAVAILABLE. Four services in the blast radius, three unmeasured edges, so caller-side detail was always going to be thin.

First check was whether the alert was real. It was. Baseline error ratio near zero (mean ~0.36%, max ~1%); inside the window the mean rose to ~3.5%, roughly tenfold, peaking near 9.2%. Onset fell inside the window, so this was not chronic. It was also not a total outage — over ninety percent of calls still succeeded — and not a blip, since threshold crossings appeared early, mid and late, the last near the window close. Note for next time: only the error-ratio series came back. No latency, restart, OOM or memory-vs-limit data was ever examined.

ad's own logs ran continuously across both ends of the window, truncated to the oldest eight and newest thirty-two lines, so the moment of onset sits in the omitted middle and was never read directly. The tail showed two failures about a minute after onset, nine seconds apart: GetAds returning UNAVAILABLE with no description and no cause. Requests either side completed cleanly; the last forty seconds showed none. No startup banner, no shutdown line, no stack trace. That ruled out a crash, a restart, a full stop in serving, and an unhandled exception in ad's own code.

## The dead ends

The log lines — UNAVAILABLE caught and returned with a null cause — read naturally as a downstream failure propagating upward. We chased that for a while and it was wrong. Traces showed ad as a leaf in every sampled trace: its only children are in-process operations finishing in fractions of a millisecond, and across one hundred and thirty-two spans there is no outbound call at all. That also killed the slow-call-cascade variant and the idea that proxy or frontend rejected requests before reaching ad. The caveat matters: no sampled trace falls inside the failing minutes and all ten succeeded, so the absence of an outbound span is established only for the healthy path. One faint inward signal — ad's server-side self-time dominates the later samples where the frontend hop dominated earlier.

flagd was the obvious downstream suspect and is clean: error ratio ~0.2% mean, under 0.7% max, slightly below its own baseline, spans emitted continuously. An order of magnitude too small to supply ad's errors.

Change history for both ad and flagd returned nothing — no deploys, image bumps, resource or config edits. But both queries ran from the incident timestamp forward about twenty-four hours, so they never covered the hours before onset. The emptiness is real but answers a different question.

Frontend logs were a third dead end: twenty-five lines spread evenly across the window, none at the moment of interest, and every line rendered as a metadata placeholder with no message body. The stream was live; it simply told us nothing about status codes or targets.

## Conclusion and what is still open

The errors originate inside ad. Tenfold elevation in its own error ratio, abrupt onset, partial and intermittent, no crash or exception, no outbound call to carry an error upward, the one plausible neighbour healthy, and no artifact change on record. What fits is a runtime-gated failure path inside ad — a value in the flag store flipped, which leaves no change-log entry. Confidence medium. Remedy class: revert the config value.

Open items. The flag store was never queried; the value and its change time are inferred from the failure's shape, not observed — close this first. Both change-history windows ran forward from onset rather than backward; re-run them over the lead-up. And trace coverage includes only successful requests outside the failing minutes, while frontend logs yielded no bodies, so caller-side impact and the other two affected services remain uncharacterised.
