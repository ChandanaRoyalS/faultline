# Cart datastore stalls absorbed as latency across the checkout path

## What we saw, and the first wrong turn

The page arrived as a warning-severity spread across twelve services, origin cartservice, with four alerting services landing within about fifteen seconds of each other: cartservice, checkoutservice, frontend, loadgenerator. That tight clustering initially read like a shared upstream problem or a network partition hitting several pods at once. It was not; it was one slow thing seen from four vantage points. Note also that four unmeasured edges were crossed on the way out, so the twelve-service figure is a reachability estimate, not twelve confirmed sick services.

The reflex was to look for failing requests. Two separate pulls of cartservice's error-status ratio came back flat at zero — 59 sample points in one, 61 in the other, min and max both zero, no spikes and no transient bursts. The ratio resolved to a defined zero rather than returning nothing, meaning the denominator was non-zero and cartservice was up and serving traced traffic throughout. That closed off both an error surge and a crash-loop. It did not close off a slow backing store, since a store that is merely slow surfaces as waiting, not as an error at the service boundary. Worth flagging: p95/p99 latency, absolute request rate, CPU/memory, throttling, and connection-pool saturation were explicitly not returned and remain unmeasured.

Logs were a partial dead end. Everything returned was INFO-level handler tracing for AddItem, GetCart, EmptyCart — no exceptions, no timeouts, and, frustratingly, no dependency named anywhere. The result was truncated to the oldest eight and newest thirty-two lines, and the truncation swallowed exactly the region covering onset, so the transition time in this record is inferred, not observed. What survived was still useful: early-window operations for a single user complete within a few milliseconds of each other, late-window ones are spread across several hundred. Same operations, same outcomes, different spacing. A handful of late GetCall entries carry an empty userId and are unexplained — possibly a client artifact, possibly upstream retry behaviour. Nothing settles it and nothing downstream depended on it.

## Traces: where the time actually went

Sampled cartservice handler spans fall into two tight bands: single-datastore-operation handlers (GetCart, EmptyCart) at roughly 301-312ms, and AddItem, which performs two operations, at roughly 603-608ms. Inside each, the datastore child span consumes essentially the whole parent — a 302ms GetCart holding a 301.3ms HGET; a 605.5ms AddItem holding a ~302.5ms HGET plus a ~302.0ms HMSET. Handler self time is one to three milliseconds, which rules out cartservice's own code, serialization, GC, or CPU pressure as the source.

The shape matters as much as the size. Every sampled datastore child sits near a common ~300ms floor with almost no spread. Queueing under load produces a long tail; this produced a wall. Total latency scales with operation count rather than payload or request type — one op ≈ 300ms, two ≈ 600ms — which is the signature of a fixed per-operation stall on the cart datastore.

Propagation is visible in the same traces and explains the alert cluster: frontend CartService calls at ~304-307ms, frontend AddItem POSTs at ~911-917ms, checkoutservice PlaceOrder at ~649-673ms with its GetCart leg ~304ms and EmptyCart leg ~303-313ms. Every caller inherits the delay unchanged.

Conclusion: cartservice is not failing, it is waiting. The mechanism is a slow dependency on the cart read/write path, absorbed as latency rather than errors. Confidence medium; indicated fix class is a configuration revert.

## The costly dead end, and what is still open

We queried cartservice's change history twice expecting to find the act that introduced the delay. Both returned nothing — no deployments, config edits, flag toggles, scaling changes, or backing-store reconfiguration. Before reading that as exculpatory, note the coverage caveat, which is the single most important limitation here: both queries covered only roughly the final fifteen minutes around onset, not the full preceding hour requested. Roughly fifty minutes before that window were never examined. What is legitimately excluded is narrow — nothing landed in the minutes immediately bracketing onset, and no rollout was mid-flight there. A change earlier in the hour remains entirely possible, and that gap is why the remediation target is inferred from the latency signature rather than read off a record.

Still open, in priority order. First: no one was dispatched to the datastore itself. The ~300ms floor was inferred entirely from the client side, and whether it originates in the Redis instance, in a proxy or sidecar on the connection path, or in a client-side setting inside cartservice determines where the fix goes. Start here. Second: go back for the unqueried earlier portion of the change window. Third: the traces alone do not distinguish a deliberate fixed delay from an instance that became uniformly slow for its own reasons — persistence, memory pressure, or a changed network path. If it is the latter, a configuration revert is the wrong remediation. Resource pressure at the cart or client layer is unmeasured rather than excluded.
