# Critical latency across six services starting at product-catalog — no cause established

## What we saw first

The page came from two places at once: the load-generator and product-catalog. Severity was called critical, with six services in the blast radius and six edges crossed that we have no measurement on. The presenting symptom was user-facing latency in the range of 0.8 to 2.2 seconds, and the starting suspicion — reasonable, given the alert pair — was that product-catalog had gone bad and the frontend was inheriting it.

That suspicion did not survive the one dispatch that returned real observations. Read this record expecting a dead end: of seven dispatches, six came back as tool errors or series that do not exist, and the single good one points away from the service we started on.

## The one piece of real evidence (T+3m)

A trace sample over the window around onset matched twenty traces and showed five of them, seventy-one spans in total. What it showed was a healthy product-catalog. Its server spans (GetProduct, ListProducts) finish in roughly 0.8ms to 9.3ms. Each one has a postgresql child completing in 0.5ms to 1.6ms, so the datastore is not holding the parent open. Where product-catalog is slowest — around seven to eight milliseconds in two traces — the time is local self time, not time waiting on the database. No product-catalog span in the sample carries an error status.

The frontend-to-product-catalog hop is clean on both sides: every frontend client span has a matching completed server child, no errors on either end. That rules out the timeout-or-aborted-call story at that hop, at least for the sampled traces.

The interesting part is where the seconds actually live. The degrading-hop breakdown attributes essentially the entire trace duration — 100% in four of the five traces — to load-generator client GET self time. The whole frontend plus product-catalog subtree occupies about 3 to 21 milliseconds, sitting at the very tail of each trace. In other words, the request spends nearly all of its life before anything we instrument starts doing work. That is an unmeasured wait on the load-generator-to-frontend edge, and we cannot see inside it.

Two caveats a future responder should carry forward. Only five of twenty matching traces were rendered and the result is marked truncated, so a rare failing product-catalog span could exist outside the sample. And the rendering surfaces no span-level error attributes at all, so even the one ERROR span we found — a load-generator client POST in a separate trace, with no product-catalog descendant and no children — cannot be read for exception detail.

## Dead end: change history (two attempts)

We went looking for what landed before onset. Both attempts died the same way: the response payload broke the JSON parser at roughly the ten-thousandth character, mid-record. This is a failure, not an empty result — nothing about the presence or absence of changes on product-catalog was established.

The only thing the failure weakly suggests is that the log did contain a non-trivial number of entries for this service and window, since an empty set would have serialized well under the size that broke it. Treat that as a hint, not a finding.

The second attempt narrowed to configuration and flag-type records on the theory that a smaller result would parse. It failed at the same character boundary, which tells us the limit is deterministic and not driven by the breadth of record types. The lever to pull next is a shorter window, pagination, or field projection — not a type filter.

Worse, and this is the part worth remembering: the window supplied on these queries began at onset and ran twenty-four hours forward. Even on success it could not have answered what changed before onset. The question and the window were mismatched. Re-run backward.

## Dead end: logs (two attempts, same broken matcher)

Both log queries were refused by the backend with an HTTP 400. The cause is in the query we sent, not in the log store: the pod label matcher used an escaped-hyphen regex construct that the matching engine will not accept. The namespace selector and the time bounds were correct and cover onset, so neither of those is implicated.

The second attempt was issued as a correction and was not actually corrected — the same escaped-hyphen pattern went out again. Nobody caught it. That cost us the entire log channel for this investigation.

Be explicit about what this does not mean. It does not mean product-catalog was silent, crashed, or dropped its output; the request was rejected before any store lookup happened, so emptiness here carries zero information. It also does not mean the log backend is unhealthy — it answered on its cluster address with a structured client error, which requires a live server that parsed and refused the request. Permissions, retention, and tenant scoping are likewise cleared, since those surface as authorization errors or successful-but-empty responses, not a parse rejection.

## Dead end: span-derived error ratios

We queried error ratios for product-catalog and then for the frontend, each against both the incident window and the preceding baseline. Both returned no samples in either window.

The symmetry is the whole point. If instrumentation had died at onset we would see baseline samples followed by a gap; instead there is nothing on either side, which means the series does not exist in this backend — not produced, or produced under a different name. So this signal cannot support a claim that either service spiked errors, and equally cannot be used to pronounce either service healthy. An empty result is not a clean bill of health.

Also note the frontend query aggregated by service name only. The frontend-to-product-catalog hop was never broken out as a dimension, so nothing here clears or implicates that hop. No status-code breakdown and no latency percentiles were retrieved either.

## Where this landed

No cause was established. Confidence is low and no fix class was identified. The honest summary is that the investigation spent itself on broken tooling and queries, and the single working signal reoriented the problem rather than solving it.

What we can say: product-catalog and its database were fast and error-free in the sampled traces, and the frontend-to-product-catalog hop was clean. What we cannot say: anything about what changed, anything about what either service logged, and anything from span-derived metrics at all.

## Pick up here

Three threads, in rough order of payoff.

First, find out where the load-generator client GET self time actually goes. That is the only place the seconds can be hiding. Candidates are connection setup, frontend accept backlog, and load-generator-side scheduling. We gathered no frontend-side logs, no resource metrics, and no ingress data — all three are unopened.

Second, reissue the log query with a valid pod matcher. A plain pattern such as pod matching product-catalog followed by any suffix will do. Until that returns, no claim about silence, exceptions, or flag text at onset is admissible.

Third, re-run change history with a window running backward from onset, and paginate or project fields so the response stays under the size that breaks parsing. The forward-running window used here could never have answered the question asked of it.
